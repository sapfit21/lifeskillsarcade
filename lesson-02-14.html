Enduring understanding and essential question

Big idea: Saving keeps your money safe. Investing puts it to work, and work can go wrong. The more a company can grow, the more it can fall. You do not get one without the other.
Essential question: How much risk is worth taking with money you cannot afford to lose?

Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. (Low) Given the eight company cards of the Gilded Age Market, describe the difference between saving and investing and sort the companies from safest to riskiest.
  2. (Mid) Given their paper ledger after Round 1, explain what each number means (cash, bank, shares, price, value, total, gain or loss) and use the trade log to check one trade.
  3. (High) Given $1,000 in 1870, decide how to split it between the bank and at least two companies and justify the split in an Investor Journal entry that names the risk level of each choice.

Vocabulary

Tier 2 (general academic): risk, reward, justify, record
Tier 3 (FACS): saving, investing, share, portfolio, ledger, total, panic. See the unit vocabulary list for the Spanish column.

Pre-assessment

The Money Basics Quiz at the end of Topic 2.3 and the Lesson 2.12 exit cards (compound growth) tell you who already knows what a savings account pays and who thinks "investing" means getting rich fast. The Day 1 do now is the second read: count how many students put the $100 in the friend's business without asking a question about the business.

Do now and hook (Time: 3 min, Day 1)

On the board: "You have $100 you will not need for a whole year. Where do you put it: a jar at home, a savings account at a bank, or your cousin's new sneaker business? One sentence: why."

Debrief: hands for jar, hands for bank, hands for the business. Then the hook: "Every one of those is a real choice adults make every month. The jar is the safest and pays nothing. The bank is almost as safe and pays a little. The business could double your money or lose all of it. For the next ten days it is 1870, you have $1,000 of paper money, and you make that choice eight times over, once for every round, from 1870 to 1932. Nobody gets hurt, and the leaderboard is real."

Questions to ask

Monitor understanding:

  • What is the difference between putting $100 in the bank line and buying two shares of RAIL with it?
  • Your total says $800. You started with $1,000. Did you lose money, or did the price of something you hold drop? Is that the same thing?
  • Find the last trade in your log. Shares times price: does it match the cash that moved?

Deepen learning:

  • Why would anyone choose the bank if it can never grow fast?
  • If a company's stock can double in a round, what else must be true about it?
  • Who in your life saves? Who invests? Do they talk about it the same way?

Check for understanding

Day 1, Step 2: clipboard yes or no per pair on "BANK sorted safest," "RADIO or AUTO sorted riskiest," and "gives a reason from the card text." Day 1, Step 3: yes or no on "two companies," "at least $100 in the bank or cash," "every buy is in the log." Day 2, Step 7: yes or no on "can name the price and value of one holding" when asked at the desk. A "got it" answer on Day 2 sounds like: "I have 8 shares of RAIL at $45, so that is $360 in value."

Closure (Time: 4 min Day 1, 4 min Day 2)

Day 1: hand out the project sheet and the rubric (or point to them in the folder) with one sentence: the leaderboard is real and is not the grade; the Investor Report is. Then Investor Journal Entry 1 (the short version: what I bought, why, and my total at the bell), then a 3-2-1 exit card: 3 things you hold (companies or the bank), 2 words that describe the risk of each, 1 thing you want to know about the next round. Two students read a "1" aloud. Sort the cards; anyone who could not name a risk level gets the risk ladder slide printed in the folder for Day 2.
Day 2: two students read their Entry 2 aloud. Exit card: "Cash, bank, shares, price, value, total: put them in a sentence about your own ledger." Sort into "got it" (all six words used correctly) and "needs more" (a second walk through the ledger at the start of Lesson 2.15).

Differentiation and supports

  • ELL: the Company Cards have a one-line description each and a ticker in capital letters; the risk ladder slide uses icons (a jar, a bank, a factory, a rocket); the journal template has sentence starters in Spanish ("Today I bought ___ because ___," "My total is $___"); pair each ELL student with a partner who can point to the line on the ledger; the words buy and sell are the only two words a trade log line needs, and slide 19 shows them in Spanish too.
  • IEP and 504 (general): the ledger has boxes, not lines; a student who cannot write three sentences can dictate to a partner; extended time by finishing Entry 2 at the start of Lesson 2.15; seat any student who gets anxious about the leaderboard where the board is not in direct view; a calculator is allowed for every total.
  • Grade 6 support: the sort uses five cards (BANK, GAS, RAIL, AUTO, RADIO), and the first buy rule is one company plus the bank, not two companies; the journal entry can be two sentences with the number.
  • Grade 8 stretch: the pair also writes one sentence predicting which company will move most by Day 5 and why, and on Day 2 computes gain or loss as a percent of 1,000 (a preview of Lesson 2.18).
  • UDL checkpoint used: multiple means of representation (the risk ladder as a drawing, the cards as text, the price table as numbers on the board) and multiple means of engagement (a real choice with a leaderboard, no right answer on Day 1).
  • No-kitchen or no-machine alternative: not needed; the whole market runs on paper and a board. If the board is in use, post the price table on chart paper and keep it up all ten days.

Homework

None. Optional: ask an adult at home one question: "Do you save, invest, or both?" Be ready to say what they said, no dollar amounts.

Connections

Math: the class math teacher can use the Day 2 gain or loss numbers as integer practice (positive and negative change) the same week; FACS gives math a real data set, math gives FACS students who can subtract from 1,000 without a calculator. Social studies: if the social studies teacher covers the Gilded Age this year, line up the weeks; FACS gives social studies students who have held a railroad share through the Panic of 1873. Community: a local bank or credit union's education officer can visit during Lesson 2.19 for the reveal.

Teacher notes

  • If Day 1 runs short, cut the debrief of the card sort to one pair and take the rest of the sort into the Day 2 do now. Never cut Round 1; a student who leaves Day 1 without living through the Panic of 1873 has nothing to journal about.
  • The order of a round never changes: prices on the board, students work out their totals, then the dispatch, then four minutes of trading. Prices before the story. If you read the story first, students trade on the story instead of learning to read the numbers.
  • The bank interest is the step students forget. Say "pay the bank first" at the start of every round from Round 1 on, and check one bank line on your walk.
  • Common mistake: students spend all $1,000 in the first two minutes on one company because it "sounds like the future." The two rules (two companies, $100 out of stock) stop the worst of it without taking the choice away. Do not fix the rest; that portfolio is a lesson on Day 5.
  • Common mistake: students read a price drop as "I lost money." Say it on Day 1 and again in October 1929: you have not lost anything until you sell. The ledger is where that becomes visible.
  • Every student starts with $1,000. Every price and date comes from the kit; do not improvise a number.
