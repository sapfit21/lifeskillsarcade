## 1. Why this unit (rationale, in Sal's voice)

At Brentwood I ran a multi-kitchen cooking project priced from a real supermarket circular. Four kitchens in one period, 112 students across four periods, $150, eleven items, every price checked against the store's own weekly ad. I wrote six costed menus, a one-page master flowchart in five-minute blocks, and a station card for each kitchen. It worked, and the reason it worked was that all of the planning was on paper before anybody turned on a burner. But I did all of that planning. The students cooked.

Before I taught, I managed food and beverage at a hotel on Fire Island. That is where I learned what a food cost actually is: the number you have to hit every week, the difference between what you planned to spend and what the register said, and the fact that somebody has to explain that difference out loud. Nobody teaches a twelve-year-old that a meal has a budget and a schedule and a supply chain behind it. They teach a recipe.

So this unit hands the planning to the students. First they trace where food comes from, count what our own room throws away, and look at who in this county cannot reach a supermarket. Then they cook four times, once as a science experiment with a variable and a control. Then they run my system themselves: pick a dish, scale it from four servings to twenty-eight, price it from this week's circular, build their own flowchart, write their own station card, hand me one shopping list that I actually buy from, cook it, serve everybody else before they serve themselves, clean the room to a signed checklist, and tell me what it really cost per person and what they would change.

I want them to walk out knowing that a meal is a supply chain, a budget, and a plan, not just a recipe. And I want them to know that the jobs in that chain are real, that several of them are twenty-five miles from here, and that two of them you can start at sixteen.

### 2.3 Trends, issues, and news

1. **Grocery prices.** Students hear adults talk about what food costs. This unit hands them the circular and makes them do the arithmetic. The number they end up with, about $2.70 to $2.80 a person for a full meal, is the most useful number in the unit, because it is theirs.
2. **Delivery apps.** Most students have watched an adult order food to the door. Lesson 6.1's chain gets one more link in the debrief, and Lesson 6.18's logistics coordinator card is the job that makes that link work. The question to ask: who pays for the driver, and is it in the price on the screen?
3. **Plant-based products.** Meat alternatives are in every supermarket now. Lesson 6.2 puts them in the local versus global discussion and Lesson 6.5 puts them in the technology discussion (FSP 4. e)). Two of the five project menu options are meatless, and that is not framed as a sacrifice.
4. **Local food and school gardens.** Farmers markets, CSA boxes, and school gardens are visible on Long Island. Lesson 6.5 plants something that is still alive on the day of Lesson 6.11, which is six weeks of care students can see.
5. **Food waste rules for businesses.** New York State requires large food scrap generators to donate excess edible food and recycle food scraps where a processor is within range. That is a real law that connects Lesson 6.3's audit to Lesson 6.4's pantries and to FSP 3. b).
6. **Climate and crops.** Students have seen a drought or a flood story affect a price. Lesson 6.1's list of factors affecting the food supply is the state's own list and it includes climate, natural disasters, and transportation. Use whatever is in the news that month.

### 2.4 What students this age find interesting

1. Cooking something they get to eat, in a room that smells like food.
2. Real money, spent on them, that they got to decide about.
3. Being in charge of a station, with a title and a job nobody else has.
4. An experiment where one thing changes and they get to find out what happens.
5. Food from their own family's kitchen being named out loud in school and treated as worth knowing.

### 2.5 Links to other subjects

- **Math:** ratio and the scale factor (6.13), unit conversion between cups, fluid ounces, and ounces by weight (6.13), unit price and unit rate (6.13, 6.15), averages and percent (6.3's waste data, 6.17's variance as a percent of plan). The four costing sheets are a ready problem set.
- **Science:** heat transfer and why dry heat browns and moist heat does not (6.6), the acid and base reaction that makes baking soda work and the complete reaction in baking powder (6.7), food spoilage and the danger zone (6.3, 6.16), and plant growth in the windowsill planting (6.5).
- **Social studies:** trade, migration, and cuisine. Lesson 6.10 takes one dish and finds it in three cultures, which is the story of trade routes and of people moving. The spice trade and the movement of staple crops between continents are the same content the social studies course teaches as history.
- **ELA:** procedural writing. The station card in Lesson 6.14 is a technical document with a test attached: another team has to be able to follow it. The dish research card in Lesson 6.10 is a short research summary with a source named. The reflection in Lesson 6.17 is a claim with evidence from the student's own documents.
- **Art:** plating and the display. Lesson 6.11's place setting and Lesson 6.17's 30 covers use composition, spacing, and the one-inch line. The menu board and the packaging redesign in Lesson 6.5 use layout and color to make something read from across the room.
- **Health:** the nutrition content stays in Unit 1 and in the health course. This unit's overlap is food safety, food access, and the cultural and social side of eating. See Section 11.

## 3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

**Big ideas:**
- Every bite of food traveled through a chain of people and places, and every link in that chain is somebody's job.
- Feeding a group is a budget and a schedule before it is a recipe. In a kitchen, time is the ingredient you cannot buy more of.
- One step always holds up everything else. Find it and build the clock around it.
- The choices a household or a school makes about food have costs beyond the price tag: fuel, water, soil, packaging, and what gets thrown away.
- Not everyone can reach the same food for the same price, and that is a system, not an accident.
- Cooking is chemistry you can eat. Change one thing on purpose and you can find out what it does.

**Essential questions:**
1. Where did this food come from, and who touched it before I did?
2. Can we feed this whole class on what I can spend, in the time we have, with the rules this room has?
3. What is the one step everything waits on?
4. What does our food actually cost, and what does it cost that is not on the receipt?
5. Who in this county cannot reach the food we just priced, and why?
6. Which job in the food chain would I actually want, and what is the shortest honest path to it?

## 4. Knowledge and skills

**Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):**
- The links in a food supply chain: producer, processor, distributor, retailer, consumer, and the cold chain that runs through all of them.
- The state's own list of factors affecting the food supply: geography, climate, economics, transportation systems, farming methods, energy, population density, political systems, natural disasters, and food waste.
- Local, seasonal, in season and out of season, food miles, farmers market, CSA, storage crop.
- Food insecurity, food desert, food pantry, SNAP, WIC, community garden, mobile market.
- Sustainability, compost, crop rotation, drip irrigation, integrated pest management, packaging, single use, shelf life; the difference between food waste and food loss.
- Dry heat and moist heat methods and what each one does to food: bake, roast, broil, toast, sauté; boil, simmer, steam, poach. Browning happens with dry heat.
- Leavening: baking powder, baking soda, the acid it needs, and the carbon dioxide that makes a quick bread rise. Variable, control, hypothesis.
- Cuisine, staple crop, flatbread, filled dough, legume, tradition, and why the same dish shows up in three cultures.
- Place setting, cover, flatware, host, shared platter, hospitality.
- Yield, portion, per serving, scale factor, purchasable unit, unit price, store brand, food cost.
- Flowchart, critical path, station card, mise en place, hold time, station check.
- The seven project constraints and the six-step sanitation routine.
- The thirteen food-chain careers, the eight links, the five training paths, and what ServSafe is.

**Skills (what students will be able to do):**
- Trace one real food from farm to plate and name the job at each link.
- Read a New York seasonal chart and build two meals, one local and seasonal and one not, and compare them on price and food miles.
- Collect and record waste data from their own room, compute a total, and scale it to a week and a year.
- Read a neighborhood map and a price comparison and decide whether an area is a food desert.
- Build a packaging prototype that uses less material, and defend it.
- Sort twelve cooking methods into dry heat and moist heat and name the tool and the right food for each.
- Run a four-batch experiment with one variable and four controls, predict, measure the rise, taste, and write a conclusion.
- Prepare and serve a pasta dish and a quesadilla dish at a station of seven inside a 40-minute period, on role cards, against the lab rubric.
- Research one dish in three cultures on a card with a source, and present it.
- Set a correct place setting and say why each piece is where it is.
- Compute a scale factor, multiply every ingredient, convert between cups, fluid ounces, and ounces by weight, round up to purchasable packages, and run a yield check against 30 portions.
- Cost a scaled recipe from a real circular with a running total, compute unit prices, find and log a store-brand swap with the dollar amount saved, and revise a plan that came in over budget.
- Build an eight-block, five-minute flowchart with a role named on every line, identify the critical path, and predict the effect of a five-minute slip as a real time.
- Write a station card with ten one-action steps, an allergen box, safety reminders, and four visible "done looks like" checks that another team can follow with no questions.
- Consolidate four costing sheets into one class shopping list sorted by store section, with a swap on every line, and pass a twelve-item approval checklist.
- Run a pre-flight check, execute a role in every block of a cook day, call the teacher at the right minute, serve 30 covers others-first, and pass a signed station check.
- Compute actual versus planned cost per person from a real receipt and explain the difference.
- Evaluate their own project against their own plan and name one change tied to a specific block, line, or step.
- Place thirteen careers on the chain and sort them by education needed, and assess personal fit with a reason.

## 5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

- **Topic 6.1:** Students will analyze where their food comes from by tracing a food from farm to plate, auditing their own room's waste, and evaluating a neighborhood's food access, then justify one sustainable practice the school could adopt.
- **Topic 6.2:** Students will apply dry heat and moist heat methods in four lab settings, design and run a controlled leavening experiment with one variable, and evaluate how culture shapes the same dish in three places.
- **Topic 6.3:** Students will design and execute a complete meal for the whole class by scaling a recipe, costing it from a real circular against a $20 station budget, building a five-minute-block flowchart with a named critical path, writing a station card another team could follow, cooking and serving it inside the period, and evaluating the plan against what actually happened and what it actually cost.
- **Topic 6.4:** Students will evaluate thirteen careers across the food chain against their own interests and the training each requires, and justify which one they would and would not want.

## 8. Project

**Name:** Feed the Class. Full sheet in `Project 06 - Feed the Class.md` (handed out in Lesson 6.12 and reread in Lesson 6.15); rubric in `Rubric 06 - Feed the Class.md`.

**The scenario:** twenty-eight people in the room and about eighty dollars. In eight class periods each station picks a dish, scales it from four servings to twenty-eight, prices it to the penny from this week's real supermarket circular, writes its own five-minute-block flowchart and its own ten-step station card, hands the teacher one consolidated shopping list that the teacher actually buys from, cooks it, serves the class before serving itself, cleans the room to a signed checklist, and reports what it really cost per person and what it would change. This is Sal's own King Kullen multi-kitchen system with the planning handed to the students.

**Deliverables:** (1) the scaled recipe with the math shown, including the unit conversions and the yield check against 30 portions; (2) the costed shopping list, the swap log, and the station's initialed rows on the approved class list; (3) the station flowchart, eight blocks, a role on every line, the critical path circled, the actual times written in after cook day; (4) the station card, ten one-action steps, an allergen box, safety reminders, four visible "done looks like" checks, passed by the paired station's read; (5) the cooked meal, served inside the period with a signed station check; (6) the four-question reflection with the cost comparison from the real receipt.

**Steps by day:** Lesson 6.12 (day 158) launch, constraints, five costed options, the choice and the justification, seven roles. Lesson 6.13 Day 1 (day 159) the scale factor, every line multiplied, unit conversions, the yield check. Lesson 6.13 Day 2 (day 160) costing from the circular, the four running totals on the wall, the swaps, the peer check, the signature. Lesson 6.14 (day 161) sticky notes into eight blocks, the critical path, the station card, the paired read, the class master grid and the collision fixes in red. Lesson 6.15 (day 162) the read-aloud consolidation, one class list by store section, unit prices and swaps, the twelve-item approval checklist, the signature. Lesson 6.16 Day 1 (day 163) Stations 1 and 2 cook while 3 and 4 prep, serve, and run the clean check. Lesson 6.16 Day 2 (day 164) reversed. Lesson 6.17 (day 165) 30 covers set, the final course served others-first, four clean zones, the waste count, the cost comparison, the reflection, the folder in.

**Group or individual:** the station team, about seven students at four stations or nine to ten at three. Seven project roles for the planning days (Station Manager, Menu Lead, Scale Lead, Cost Lead, Flow Lead, Shopping Lead, Reflection Lead) and the room's standard lab roles on cook day (Station Manager, Head Cook, Second Cook, Prep Cook, Helper Cook, Plate Builder, Time Keeper, plus Toppings Chief at eight). Nobody holds the same job in both.

**Choice built in:** the dish, from five costed options plus a sixth the team may propose and price itself; every store-brand and size swap; the whole flowchart, including which shortcut to take if the plan slips; the station card format, written or drawn as the picture version; the reflection format, written, typed, dictated, recorded, or drawn as a marked-up flowchart; and the language of the host's opening sentence at the table.

**ELL and IEP supports:** the picture flowchart card (eight drawn boxes with symbols) counts as a real station card; sentence starters for the choice, the swap log, the flowchart, and the reflection in Turkish, Portuguese, and Spanish, all to be verified by a native speaker; a calculator for every student on every day; a pre-filled scaling table with the factor already entered and a four-page circular excerpt with only this project's items; the first two rows of the costing sheet and the consolidated list worked as examples; a fixed physical position for every lab role, written on the duty strip and kept in the student's pocket; Plate Builder, Time Keeper, and the prep station as burner-free roles available to anyone on both cook days at no rubric cost; a written lab alternative for a student absent on a cook day. Full list in `Project 06`, Supports.

**How it is presented:** the meal itself is the presentation. Thirty covers set properly, the allergen box read to the class, the class served before the cooks serve themselves, and a real receipt projected on the wall while students compute what they actually spent.

**Full no-kitchen version:** the meal becomes a no-cook assembled meal (a wrap bar, a snack board, overnight oats, a salsa and bean side, or the dessert cups), five options priced for 28 portions at station scale, and the rest of the project is unchanged: the same eight days, the same six deliverables, the same budget, the same rubric, the same flowchart with assembly phases instead of cook phases, the same station card, the same pre-flight and duty list, the same 30 covers, the same four clean zones, the same reflection. What is lost is the second performance of FSP 1. e) with heat. What is gained is a harder cold-chain and hold-time problem. Say that out loud so the no-kitchen version does not read as the consolation prize. Full menu and prices in `Project 06`.

## 11. Health and other-department coordination

**Science.** Two places, and both are real opportunities rather than courtesies. **The leavening lab (6.7)** is a controlled experiment with one variable and four controls; the science teacher's words for variable, control, hypothesis, and conclusion should be the words on the lab sheet, so ask which wording the grade 7 and grade 8 science classes use and match it. **The growing in 6.5** is a plant life cycle with light, water, and soil as the inputs, and the cups sit on the windowsill for six weeks, which is longer than most science units can hold a living thing. FACS gives science a four-batch data set with a measurable outcome and a windowsill; science gives FACS the vocabulary and, if willing, five minutes on Day 2 of Lesson 6.7 to look at the four batches.

**Math.** Lesson 6.13 is ratio, unit conversion, and unit rate doing real work, and Lesson 6.15 is unit price. Use the same setup and the same words the math class uses: the scale factor as a ratio, the price per ounce as a unit rate, and the variance in Lesson 6.17 as percent change (end minus start over start). FACS gives math four real costing sheets, a set of unit-rate problems with a store circular attached, and the class's own cost-per-person number; math gives FACS the method students already know and, if willing, ten minutes on Lesson 6.13 Day 2 to check the four running totals.

**The cafeteria manager.** Three things, and this is the most useful relationship in the unit. **The waste audit (6.3):** the audit is more honest with the manager's cooperation, and the manager may already have a number. **The allergy list:** the building already runs an allergen protocol and the manager knows it; the class allergy list for Lessons 6.7 through 6.17 should match what the cafeteria works from, and the nurse signs off on it. **Purchasing (6.15):** five minutes on how the school actually buys food, what a case costs against a retail package, and what the school's real per-meal figure is. FACS gives the manager a class that can read a unit price and a purchase list and a waste number from their own room; the manager gives FACS the building's numbers and its protocol.

**Health.** This unit does not teach nutrition. Unit 1 taught the six nutrients, MyPlate, and labels, and the health course owns dietary guidance, body image, and eating disorders. Unit 6's overlap with health is three narrow things: food safety and the danger zone (already taught in Unit 1 and practiced here), food access and food insecurity (Lesson 6.4, where the health course may also cover community health resources), and the cultural and social side of eating (Lessons 6.10, 6.11, 6.17). Anything a student says in Lesson 6.4 about food insecurity at home is a quiet referral to the counselor, not a class discussion, and the lesson says so.

**Social studies.** If the social studies course covers trade routes, migration, or the movement of crops between continents, Lesson 6.10 is the same content from the plate side. Share the dish research card. FACS stops at what people eat and why; the causes and the chronology belong to social studies.

**ELA.** The station card in Lesson 6.14 is procedural writing with a test attached, and the reflection in Lesson 6.17 is a claim with evidence. Share both templates so students see one structure for a procedure and one for an argument.

## 12. Connections (in-building and community)

**Inside the building:**
- **The cafeteria manager**, for Lessons 6.3, 6.15, and 6.17. **FACS gives:** the waste number from the class's own audit and again from the project's Zone 4 count, a class that can read a unit price and a purchase list, and thirty students who now know what the kitchen staff actually do. **FACS gets:** the building's real per-meal cost, the allergen protocol the school already runs, five minutes on case pricing versus retail pricing, and cooperation on the audit.
- **The science teacher**, for Lessons 6.5 and 6.7. **FACS gives:** a four-batch controlled experiment with a measurable rise, a windowsill of living plants that lasts six weeks, and a lab sheet using science's own vocabulary. **FACS gets:** the department's wording for variable, control, and hypothesis, and, if willing, five minutes in the room on the day the batches come out of the oven.
- **The math teacher**, for Lessons 6.13 and 6.15 (see Section 11).
- **The custodian and the building engineer**, for Lessons 6.16 and 6.17. A two-minute conversation the week before about a four-burner, full-oven day and a room that gets cleaned to a signed checklist, and in exchange a working exhaust fan, an extra bus tub, and a warning if the hot water is out.

**Community:**
- **A supermarket manager** (the store the circular comes from), for Lessons 6.12, 6.13, and 6.15. **FACS gives:** a class of customers who can read a unit price, a thank-you note from every student, and a real purchase. **FACS gets:** a stack of this week's circulars, a five-minute answer on why a store brand costs less and who writes the circular, sometimes a donation toward the $80, and occasionally permission to bring two students along on the shopping trip.
- **A Cornell Cooperative Extension of Suffolk County educator**, for Lessons 6.5, 6.4, and 6.18. **FACS gives:** a class that has audited its own waste, planted something, and studied food access, plus a ready audience for a program Extension already runs in schools. **FACS gets:** a professional who can talk about local growing, composting, food safety, and nutrition, materials that are free and already written for this age, and a real person behind the farmer, agricultural engineer, and dietitian cards. This is the easiest yes on the list.
- **A local farm or farmers market vendor** on the North Fork or at the nearest market, for Lessons 6.2 and 6.18. **FACS gives:** twenty-eight families who now know what "in season" means and where the market is, and a thank-you from the class. **FACS gets:** ten minutes on what actually grows here and when, what a grower is paid compared to the shelf price, and one story about a season that went wrong.
- **A food pantry or food rescue organization**, for Lessons 6.4 and 6.17. **FACS gives:** unopened, in-date, shelf-stable leftovers where the pantry's rules allow it, a class that has counted its own waste, and students who know what SNAP and WIC are. **FACS gets:** one visit or one email answering what they wish more people understood about feeding a group, and real numbers about need in this county.
