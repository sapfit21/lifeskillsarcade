# Unit 7: Launch: Career Readiness and FACS Shark Tank

**NYS content module:** none of the six is primary. This is the capstone, and it is built on **Individual Growth and Life Readiness topic 3 (Employability)** and **topic 5 (Career Pathways)**, plus the Career Pathways topic that every one of the six content modules carries and that this course has been teaching in one careers lesson per unit all year.
**Length:** 13 class periods of 40 minutes (days 168 to 180 of the year, inside marking period 4). Fourteen periods is the better version; see Section 6.
**CTE themes carried:** Career and Community Opportunities (primary); Communication and Interpersonal Relationships; Problem Solving and Innovation; Financial and Consumer Literacy; Health, Safety, and Wellness; Sustainability
**Unit project:** FACS Shark Tank, scored with `Rubric 07 - Career Readiness and Shark Tank.md`
**Companion files in this folder:** `Calendar 07.md`, `Vocabulary 07.md`, Lessons 7.1 to 7.12, `Project 07 - FACS Shark Tank.md`, `Rubric 07 - Career Readiness and Shark Tank.md`. There is no `Assessment 07.x` file and no unit test; the course grading plan makes the capstone project and the year reflection the final. Handouts and slide outlines are in.

Reconciliation note: this plan was written to the scope and sequence and then reconciled to every lesson, the project sheet, and the rubric as they landed in the folder. Where a lesson page and this plan disagree, the lesson page wins; the calendar and this plan follow the lessons' day counts, handout names, and check slots.

## 1. Why this unit

I have sat on both sides of the interview table. I have been the one sweating in the chair and I have been the one doing the hiring. I have a degree in Corporate Innovation and Entrepreneurship and an MBA. I did business development at a software company, I held a financial services license, and I ran food and beverage at a hotel where every week ended with somebody explaining a number out loud. I also designed and coded a web app, ClassroomStreet, by myself, and my own students used it to practice budgeting with real expenses. So when I tell a thirteen-year-old that the way you talk about yourself is a skill and not a personality trait, I am not reading it off a slide.

I have built resumes one on one with more than eighty-seven students. Most of them told me they had nothing to put on one. Every single one of them was wrong, and the proof was sitting in a folder in my room. That is the idea this unit runs on. By the time a student walks into Unit 7, they have cooked and served a dish for a station of seven inside a forty-minute period, passed a safety exam, sewn or mended something, designed a room to scale, built a kit for a four-year-old, planned and priced a meal for thirty people from a real supermarket circular, and traded a portfolio in a simulation. That is not a list of activities. That is a resume, and this unit is where a student finds that out.

Then we go the other way. For seven days students stop being the person applying and become the person deciding. They find a real problem in one of the six things this class covers, ask three actual people whether they would pay to solve it, work out what one unit costs and what they would charge and how many they have to sell before they stop losing money, build something a person can hold, and pitch it in sixty seconds to four adults who did not have to come. Then those adults ask them a question nobody warned them about, and they answer it.

Two weeks is not long enough to teach anybody to run a business. It is long enough to prove to a thirteen-year-old that they can stand in front of an adult they do not know, with something they made, and say a number out loud. I have watched students who spent half the week in in-school suspension do exactly that. This is the unit I would want a principal to walk into.

### 2.2 How the CTE themes come in

- **Career and Community Opportunities (primary).** This is the theme the whole unit is built on, and it is the only unit in the course where CCO topic 3 (Employability Skills) and topic 4 (Career Plans) are the content rather than a thread. CCO 3. a) is Lesson 7.1's folder mining and Lesson 7.4's anchor chart; 3. c) is the resume and the four traits named out loud; 3. d) is the whole of Topic 7.1, because the line says "demonstrate personal development of employability skills through practice of these skills in a variety of classroom applications" and a mock interview with a scored feedback sheet is that line word for word. CCO 4. a) and 4. b) are Lesson 7.12's reading of the seven careers self-checks; 4. d) is the grade 8 stretch in Lesson 7.12. CCO 1. The Work World a) and b) are Lesson 7.6, where a business is defined as a problem somebody pays to solve and work is what gets paid for. CCO 5. c) is the option, built into `Project 07`, that a team's idea serve a real community need.
- **Communication and Interpersonal Relationships.** Second in weight and it never lets up. CIR 1. f) is Lessons 7.3, 7.4, 7.9, and 7.11; CIR 2. c) and 2. d) are the observer role in Lesson 7.5, which is the only place in the course where a student is graded on listening; CIR 3. a) is the difference between how you talk to a friend and how you talk to a manager, taught in Lesson 7.4; CIR 3. e) is the resume and the numbers page as written reports somebody else has to act on; CIR 3. f) is every team period in Topic 7.2; CIR 6. a) is the Lesson 7.3 self-score and CIR 6. f) is the applaud-everyone-the-same rule on pitch day.
- **Problem Solving and Innovation.** The design process is the structure of Topic 7.2, and it runs in the state's own order: PSI 2. a) a. defining the problem (Lesson 7.7), b. defining criteria (Lesson 7.8's customer profile), c. defining constraints (Lesson 7.7's five-day buildability filter), d. brainstorming (Lesson 7.7), e. selecting the best solution (Lesson 7.7's filter and ranking), f. building a prototype (Lesson 7.10), g. testing it against the criteria (Lesson 7.10 Day 2's rehearsal and peer review), i. evaluating their use of the process (Lesson 7.12's reflection). PSI 1. d) is innovation defined as a new application of something that already exists, which is what most middle school ideas actually are and it is worth naming. PSI 1. e) is pitch day.
- **Financial and Consumer Literacy.** FCL 4. a) is Lesson 7.6's definition of a consumer and their role; FCL 4. c) is the numbers page, which is a spending plan for one product; FCL 1. a) and 1. b) sit behind the resume and the career line in Lesson 7.12, where income potential is what makes a career choice a financial choice. This is the theme Unit 2 owns; here it appears as the arithmetic behind an idea rather than as personal finance.
- **Health, Safety, and Wellness.** The lightest of the six here, and honest about it. HSW 1. f) is the line that fits: how an individual's health status affects performance of tasks at home, at school, and in workplace and community settings. It comes up in Lesson 7.3, where composure under pressure is partly a physical thing (breathing, sleep, nerves) and students should hear that said plainly. The lab safety thread that ran through six units has no new content here; any team building a food prototype runs the Unit 1 allergen procedure and the teacher operates the box cutter, the iron, and the sewing machine.
- **Sustainability.** Also light and also honest. SUS 1. b), sustainability as a factor in decision making, is available to any team whose idea reuses, repairs, or reduces waste, and after Unit 5's upcycle challenge and Unit 6's waste audit, at least two teams a year pick one. It is offered in `Project 07`, not required.

### 2.3 Trends, issues, and news

1. **Artificial intelligence and entry-level work.** Students have used a chatbot and they have heard an adult say a computer is going to take somebody's job. The honest thing to tell a thirteen-year-old: the tasks most easily automated are the ones nobody has to be in a room for, and the skills this unit teaches (standing in front of a person, answering a question you did not plan for, deciding under a deadline, working with two other people who disagree with you) are the ones that are hardest to hand to software. Also true and worth saying: a resume now often gets read by software before a person sees it, which is why specific words and real numbers matter. Lesson 7.1 says so.
2. **The creator and side-hustle economy.** Most students in the room know somebody who sells something online, streams, resells sneakers, does nails, cuts hair, or drives for an app. That is entrepreneurship and it deserves to be named as such in Lesson 7.6, including the parts nobody posts: the cost per unit, the hours, and whether it clears a real profit. Lesson 7.6's business-versus-hobby distinction lands hard here and it should never be delivered as a put-down.
3. **Apprenticeships and the trades.** New York has registered apprenticeship programs and a student can begin a path at sixteen to eighteen in trades that pay well and require no four-year degree. Unit 6's careers lesson already named ServSafe as a credential a high schooler can earn. Lesson 7.12's career line should offer the trades as a first-class answer, not a fallback.
4. **Skills-based hiring over degree requirements.** A number of large employers have dropped degree requirements for some roles in favor of demonstrated skills. That is directly relevant to a unit whose deliverable is a portfolio of demonstrated skills, and it is the one trend that makes this unit's argument for it: a folder that proves what you can do is worth something on its own.
5. **The interview that is a video call.** A first interview is now often a video call, sometimes a recorded one with no human on the other end. Everything in Lessons 7.4 and 7.5 still applies and three things change: where you look (the camera, not the screen), what is behind you, and the fact that silence is longer and more uncomfortable on a call than in a room. My original version of the Lesson 7.4 material was delivered over video, so I have taught it both ways. Worth two minutes in Lesson 7.5's debrief.
6. **Prices, wages, and what a first job pays.** Students hear adults talk about what things cost. A minimum wage number, a first-job hourly figure, and a monthly total is arithmetic they can do and it is what makes Lesson 7.6's profit example feel like it is about them.

### 2.4 What students this age find interesting

1. Money that is real, and being the one who decides what to charge.
2. Being taken seriously by an adult who is not their teacher and does not have to be there.
3. Their own name on something. The folder, the poster, the award card.
4. Winning something in a category that is theirs. Best Numbers is a real win for a student who will never win Best Pitch.
5. Finding out that what they already do (watching a sibling, cooking dinner, translating for a parent, cutting hair on a Saturday) counts as experience and has a name.
6. A question with no right answer, asked fast, where being funny is allowed.

### 2.5 Links to other subjects

- **Math.** The most direct link in the unit and it should be coordinated by name. Unit cost is a sum. Margin is a difference. **Break-even is a division whose answer must be rounded up to a whole item,** which is a genuinely good remainder-means-something problem. The one-month projection is a two-step expression. Panel 4 is a data display that has to be readable from ten feet. Ask the math teacher which words they use for unit rate at grade 7 and use theirs. The ten teams' numbers pages are a ready problem set for a math class in June.
- **ELA.** Three pieces of real writing with real audiences: the resume (a compressed claim with evidence and no adjectives), the 60-second pitch (persuasion with a fixed structure and a hard time limit), and the year reflection (a claim supported by evidence the student physically holds). NYS Next Generation grade 8 speaking and listening standards on presenting claims with evidence and adapting speech to context are what Lessons 7.4, 7.5, 7.9, and 7.11 do; My own version of the Lesson 7.4 material cited 8SL4, 8SL6, and 8W4 and those are the right ones.
- **Art.** The five-panel deck is layout, hierarchy, and legibility at distance. The prototype is a model. Two minutes from the art teacher on "what does a person read first on this poster" is worth more than ten minutes from a FACS teacher.
- **Social studies.** Markets, work, and the role of the consumer. Lesson 7.6's problem-first definition of a business is the household-scale version of supply and demand, and CCO 7. c) (how social, economic, and technological changes change work) is a social studies question being asked in a FACS room.
- **Science and technology.** The design process in Lesson 7.7 and Lesson 7.10 is the same process a technology or engineering class runs. Match the vocabulary on purpose; students should hear the same words in both rooms.
- **Health.** No overlap to coordinate in this unit. The one connection is the honest one in Lesson 7.3: nerves, sleep, and breathing affect how you perform in front of people, which is HSW 1. f) and which the health course may cover as stress management. Unit 3 already taught the stress toolkit; refer back to it rather than reteaching it.

## 3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

**Big ideas:**
- The way you talk about yourself is a skill, not a personality trait. It has a structure, the structure can be learned, and the people who sound confident are the ones who practiced.
- A resume is not a list of jobs. It is proof, in writing, that you have already done things, and a year of ordinary schoolwork is full of proof.
- When somebody asks you a question you cannot prepare for, nobody is grading the answer. They are watching how you handle not knowing.
- A business exists because somebody has a problem they will pay to have solved. No problem, no business.
- You do not get to decide whether your idea is good. Your customer does, and you find out by asking before you build.
- Numbers decide. An idea everyone likes and nobody can afford is not a business, and finding that out on paper is the cheapest way it will ever happen.
- An idea somebody can hold is worth more than an idea somebody has to imagine.
- You cannot prove you learned something by saying so. You prove it by pointing at what you made.

**Essential questions:**
1. What have I already done that somebody would want to pay me for?
2. What is somebody actually measuring when they ask me a question I cannot prepare for?
3. Why can I not just wing it when somebody says "tell me about yourself"?
4. What problem is this business actually solving, and who is paying to have it solved?
5. How many do we have to sell before we stop losing money?
6. What would make somebody who has never met me want to hear the rest?
7. What can I do now that I could not do in September, and where is the proof?

## 4. Knowledge and skills

**Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):**
- The seven sections of a middle school resume, who reads a resume and for about how long, and the rule that everything on it is true and specific.
- The three-part resume bullet: the work, the explanation, the output.
- The four things an employer watches during an unexpected question: composure, communication, problem solving, adaptability, and one observable sign of each.
- The three parts of a "tell me about yourself" answer: who you are, what you are good at, why you fit.
- STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result, and that the Result is the part people skip.
- The professional opening and closing of an interview: greeting, name, handshake, sitting when invited, thank you, and follow up.
- The four questions every business answers: what problem, who has it, what do you make or do, what does it cost and what do you charge.
- Product and service; customer and target customer; the fact that the payer is not always the user.
- Revenue, cost, unit cost, price, margin, profit, break-even, projection, and the difference between a business and a hobby.
- The customer profile's five specifics, including what the customer already does about the problem, which is the real competition.
- The six parts of a 60-second pitch and the seconds each gets: hook 10, problem 10, solution 15, who it is for 10, the money 10, the ask 5.
- The five panels of a pitch deck: the problem, the solution, the customer, the numbers, the ask.
- What a prototype is, and that a drawing, a mock-up, or a written card counts.
- The three C-suite roles and what each owns: CEO the idea, the pitch, and the clock; CFO every number; CMO the customer, the look, and the prototype.
- The forty-five Tier 3 terms in `Vocabulary 07.md`.

**Skills (what students will be able to do):**
- Mine a year of their own work for evidence and name what an employer would care about in each piece.
- Write a resume bullet in three parts with a number, a quantity, or a named audience in it.
- Produce a one-page resume with seven sections, no paragraphs, and nothing on it they cannot point to.
- Give and act on specific feedback against a written checklist.
- Deliver a 30-second answer to an unexpected question that stays on the question, states a decision, and gives a reason.
- Score themselves on four observable traits with evidence and name one to work on.
- Deliver a three-part "tell me about yourself" answer that names a provable skill.
- Tell one STAR story from their own year with a Result that has a number, a time, or a named outcome in it.
- Greet, shake hands, answer seven interview questions, and thank an interviewer.
- Observe an interview and write two pieces of feedback that name what was seen or heard.
- Find the problem behind a real business and say who has it.
- Compute revenue, cost, and profit, and separate cost per unit from total cost.
- Generate twenty ideas without evaluating them, then filter them against three criteria and rank the survivors.
- Build a customer profile in five specifics and conduct three customer interviews recorded in the customer's own words.
- Compute unit cost line by line, set a price from customer evidence, compute margin per unit, compute break-even in units rounded up, and build a one-month projection with a defended unit count.
- Say a break-even as a sentence out loud, under pressure, without looking at the paper.
- Draft, time, and cut a 60-second pitch in six parts.
- Build a five-panel deck whose numbers panel is readable from ten feet.
- Build a prototype using a skill from the year and name which unit it came from.
- Rehearse standing against a timer and revise in response to peer review, naming each change and its reason.
- Deliver a pitch to adults they do not know and answer an unplanned question with a number or a quote.
- Score ten teams against four criteria and defend an award vote with evidence.
- Assemble a twelve-item portfolio and write one line per artifact naming the skill it proves.
- Write a year reflection with evidence and name one skill they will use first and when.

## 5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

- **Topic 7.1:** Students will evaluate a year of their own work for evidence of employable skill, create a one-page resume in which every bullet has the work, the explanation, and the output, and demonstrate a professional interview in which they deliver a three-part self-introduction and one STAR story with a Result, then justify one revision to their own answer using feedback a peer gave them about something the peer actually saw or heard.
- **Topic 7.2:** Students will design a business from a real problem they found themselves by evaluating twenty of their own ideas against three criteria, validating the chosen one with three customer interviews and revising it in response, computing unit cost, price, margin, and break-even in units, constructing a prototype with a skill from this course and a five-panel deck whose numbers are readable at distance, and defending the whole thing in a 60-second pitch to an adult panel including an answer to a question they did not plan for.

**A note on Lesson 7.8 and the thirteen-day envelope.** Lesson 7.8 has two periods of content and is written that way: Day 1 the customer, Day 2 the money. The year calendar gives Unit 7 thirteen periods, which is exactly enough for twelve lessons if only Lesson 7.10 runs two days. So in the thirteen-day calendar Lesson 7.8 runs one period, using the one-period procedure printed in the lesson, and the teams' own numbers pages are completed in the **Numbers Clinic that opens Lesson 7.10 Day 1**, which is built into that lesson's timed procedure and is not an improvisation. **If your school's calendar gives Unit 7 fourteen periods, run Lesson 7.8 across two days and shift everything after it one cell right.** That is the better unit and it is the version to ask for, because the fourteenth period buys a full class period on unit cost, margin, and break-even, which is the most transferable arithmetic in the course.

**Flex lessons (drop first when time runs short), in this order:**
1. **Lesson 7.3, ACT FAST** (day 170). Fold the four traits into Lesson 7.4's anchor chart as a five-minute block and give the self-score sheet as that lesson's exit card. The drill is the best day of Topic 7.1 for classroom climate and the least load-bearing for the rubric, which is why it is first. Saves 1 day.
2. **Lesson 7.6, What a Business Is** (day 173). Fold the four questions and the profit example into Lesson 7.7's do now and first block, using two businesses instead of five. Saves 1 day, and it costs the revenue, cost, and profit teaching that Lesson 7.8 depends on, so only do this if Unit 2's money work was strong.
3. **Lesson 7.10 Day 2** (day 178). Rehearse twice inside Day 1's last twelve minutes and run the peer review as a written swap of the peer review sheet instead of a delivered pitch. Saves 1 day and it costs pitch quality directly, which is visible to the guests, so it is third.

## 8. Project

**Name:** FACS Shark Tank. Full sheet in `Project 07 - FACS Shark Tank.md` (handed out in Lesson 7.7 and reread in Lesson 7.10 Day 1); rubric in `Rubric 07 - Career Readiness and Shark Tank.md`.

**The scenario:** three students, one business idea, seven class periods. On the last Friday of the school year, four adults sit at a table in the FACS room: the teacher, an administrator, another teacher, and one adult who does not work in the building. Each team gets 60 seconds to pitch and then 90 seconds of questions. The numbers have to be real. The customers have to be people the team actually asked. The prototype has to be something they actually made. The one rule of the project is that it cannot be made up.

**The FACS rule:** the idea has to connect to at least one of the six things the course covered (food, money, family, home, clothing, work) and the team has to say which and why in one sentence. That rule is what makes this a Family and Consumer Sciences capstone instead of a business elective, and it is why a student who spent the year learning to sew a hem, cost a meal, and measure a room has an advantage.

**Deliverables:** (1) the one-page business brief with the problem, who has it, the product or service, one differentiator, and the module connection sentence; (2) three customer interviews recorded in the customer's own words plus the line naming what they changed; (3) the numbers page, signed by the CFO: unit cost line by line, price, margin per unit, fixed cost, break-even in units, and a one-month projection with a defended unit count; (4) the five-panel deck or poster, the problem, the solution, the customer, the numbers, the ask; (5) the prototype or sample, built with a skill from this year, with the unit named; (6) the 60-second pitch in six parts, delivered by all three members.

**Steps by day:** Lesson 7.6 (day 173) the four questions, the problem behind five real businesses, revenue and cost and profit, business or hobby. Lesson 7.7 (day 174) the six-area problem scan, twenty ideas with no criticism, the three-question filter, the choice, the module sentence, the roles. **Gate: the signed filter sheet.** Lesson 7.8 (day 175) the customer profile, three rotating customer interviews, what changed, and the whole-class numbers worked example. Lesson 7.9 (day 176) the six pitch parts, the model pitch critiqued, the draft, timed twice. Lesson 7.10 Day 1 (day 177) the Numbers Clinic, the panel plan, panels 1 to 3, the prototype. **Gate: the signed planner sheet and the signed numbers page.** Lesson 7.10 Day 2 (day 178) panels 4 and 5 finished, three timed standing rehearsals, the team-to-team peer review, two written revisions, **the folder in and criteria 3, 4, and 5 scored.** Lesson 7.11 (day 179) pitch day. **Criterion 6 scored live.** Lesson 7.12 (day 180) the project materials go into each student's own portfolio.

**Group or individual:** teams of three, assigned by the teacher, with three roles. **CEO** owns the idea, the pitch, and the clock. **CFO** owns every number and signs the numbers page. **CMO** owns the customer interviews, the panel layout, and the prototype. All three know all four numbers and all three speak part of the pitch. Nobody is the boss of the other two, and that sentence is printed on the role cards because it is the argument the teacher would otherwise referee on the build days.

**Choice built in:** the problem, found in the team's own scan; product or service; which FACS module it connects to and how; what the prototype is, from a menu of seven routes that each use a different unit's skill; the price; the ask; poster or trifold or five taped sheets; who says which part of the pitch and in what proportion; and which artifact from the project goes in each student's own portfolio.

**ELL and IEP supports:** the picture panel template for all five panels, offered to every team and not assigned to one; customer interviews conducted and recorded in Turkish, Portuguese, or Spanish with one line translated for panel 3, and a quote left in the customer's own language scored at the same level; sentence starters in all four languages for the six pitch parts, the three interview questions, the break-even sentence, and the module sentence, all to be verified by a native speaker; **a written pitch part read aloud by a teammate, as a named accommodation that does not cap the rubric score**; a pitch delivered to the judge table with no audience; a recorded or written pitch scored on the same descriptors; calculators for everyone with no permission needed; pre-labeled numbers page, panel planner, and interview sheet; teacher-operated box cutter, iron, and sewing machine for every student; extended build time at lunch on both Lesson 7.10 days; teams of three assigned by the teacher so nobody is picked last. Full list in `Project 07`, and the corresponding rubric language is in `Rubric 07`'s how-to-use note.

**How it is presented:** pitch day, `Lesson 07.11`. Four adults at a judge table, a running order posted, a student timekeeper, 60 seconds and 90 seconds of questions per team, every student in the audience scoring every team on a four-criteria sheet so nobody spends 25 minutes watching, and four awards in named categories: **Most Useful, Best Numbers, Best Pitch, Most Likely To Actually Work.** The judges announce their winners; the class's own votes are read afterward and the difference between the two is named out loud as a normal thing. **No-guest version:** the panel becomes the teacher plus three students from teams that already pitched, with the same judge sheet, running in two halves with a swap at the midpoint. Do not cancel pitch day for lack of guests.

**Full no-lab version:** the project requires no kitchen and no sewing machine as written. A food idea becomes a costed menu card with a drawing or photograph; an apparel idea becomes a hand-sewn sample, a drawing with fabric swatches taped on, or a mended item from home. A room with tables, paper, rulers, and the Unit 5 scrap bin can run this project complete. What is lost is a second performance of a machine skill; what is gained is that every team's prototype is finishable inside two periods, which is why the drawing route is on the menu for everyone and not just for a room with no lab.

## 11. Health and other-department coordination

**Math.** The most important coordination in the unit and the easiest to arrange. Lesson 7.8's numbers page uses a sum (unit cost), a difference (margin), a division with a rounded-up whole-number answer (break-even in units), and a two-step expression (the projection). Ask the grade 7 math teacher two questions in May: **which words do you use for unit rate,** and **have they done a division where the remainder forces you to round up.** Use their words. FACS gives math ten real numbers pages as a June problem set and one authentic division whose remainder means something you cannot sell. Math gives FACS the vocabulary students already have and, if willing, ten minutes in Lesson 7.10 Day 1's Numbers Clinic checking arithmetic, which is the single most useful adult help available in this unit.

**ELA.** Three pieces of real writing in twelve days. Tell the ELA teacher in May which week the resume, the pitch, and the reflection happen. The resume is a compressed claim with evidence and no adjectives; the pitch is persuasion inside a hard time limit; the reflection is a claim supported by evidence the student is physically holding. FACS gives ELA authentic writing with a real audience and a real deadline; ELA gives FACS the revision language students already know and, if willing, one period of the resume conference rotation, which doubles how many students get a two-minute conference.

**Art.** Two minutes in Lesson 7.10 Day 1 on what a person reads first on a poster, and whether a number is legible from ten feet. FACS gives art a real design constraint with an audience; art gives FACS a two-minute lesson a FACS teacher cannot deliver as well.

**The school counselor.** Three things. **Lesson 7.5:** the counselor can take one triad and run four real high school admissions interviews, which raises the stakes for the whole room. **Lesson 7.12:** the counselor should have a copy of the portfolio checklist, because a student who walks into a high school conversation with that folder is a different applicant. **Records:** the counselor keeps activities and awards records a student will not remember for the resume in Lesson 7.1.

**Administration.** This is the one unit where an administrator's presence is part of the curriculum, not a courtesy. Ask in writing, in September, for **45 minutes on the last Friday of the year.** The ask is small and the return is that an administrator watches ten teams defend real arithmetic in front of them.

**Health.** No coordination needed. Unit 3 taught stress and the stress toolkit; Lesson 7.3's one health connection (nerves and physical composure) refers back to it rather than reteaching it.

## 12. Connections (in-building and community)

**Inside the building:**
- **Math teacher.** FACS gives: ten authentic numbers pages, a rounded-up division, and a class of students who have a reason to care about unit rate in June. FACS gets: matching vocabulary and ten minutes of arithmetic checking in the Lesson 7.10 Numbers Clinic.
- **ELA teacher.** FACS gives: three real pieces of writing with real audiences and deadlines, and evidence that persuasive structure transfers. FACS gets: revision language, confirmed standard codes, and possibly a second adult for the resume conference rotation.
- **School counselor.** FACS gives: a class already trained in the three-part answer and the STAR frame, and a portfolio that makes a high school conversation easier. FACS gets: real admissions questions, the activities and awards records, and an adult interviewer for Lesson 7.5.
- **Administrator.** FACS gives: 25 minutes of direct evidence about what students in the building can do, with a judge sheet in their hand. FACS gets: the fact that the audience is real, which is what makes students rehearse.
- **Another teacher, any subject.** FACS gives: a seat at the judge table and a genuinely good 45 minutes. FACS gets: a second adult voice, and a colleague who now knows what happens in the FACS room.
- **Art and technology teachers.** As in Section 11.

**Community, and the guest panel logistics in full:**

This is the only unit in the course whose central event depends on outside adults, so the logistics are written out rather than left to the week of.

- **Who to ask, in priority order:** one administrator (principal, assistant principal, or dean); one teacher who is not the FACS teacher; one parent or guardian, preferably one who runs a business or works in hiring; one outside adult, which can be a local small business owner, a bank branch employee, a chamber of commerce member, a school board member, or a graduate of the school. **Four is the target. Three is enough. One outside adult in year one is a success.**
- **When to ask: September.** Not June. Put the date on the school calendar with the rest of the year's events. A business owner can hold 45 minutes nine months out and cannot hold it nine days out.
- **What to ask for, in writing:** "45 minutes on [date] at [time] to hear ten teams of eighth graders pitch a business they built, and to ask each one question. You will get a one-page briefing and a score sheet. You do not need to prepare anything."
- **What to send two days ahead:** the one-page judge briefing from `Handout 07.11`, the running order, and the six parts of the pitch so judges know the structure students were taught. A judge who knows the structure asks a question inside it.
- **What to hand them at the door, five minutes early:** the packet of judge sheets with the running order on the front, water, a chair at the table, and three spoken instructions: score every team, ask one question per team inside the 90 seconds, and say one specific true thing to every team before the next one starts.
- **The one thing to tell them plainly:** their sheets are not the grade of record. It frees them to write honestly and it protects students from a stranger's off day.
- **The failure mode to manage:** a judge who gives a two-minute speech instead of asking a question. The briefing says one question and ten seconds of feedback. Stand up at second 80 and they will wrap.
- **Afterward:** thank them by name, out loud, in front of the students, and send a handwritten note from the class. Keep the list. The same people come back the next year and they bring somebody.
- **What the community gives and gets:** FACS gives a local employer 45 minutes of direct contact with the students who will apply to them in three years, and a genuinely interesting hour. FACS gets an audience that students will rehearse for, questions a teacher cannot ask with the same weight, and a relationship that pays off in guest speakers, a summer job lead, and a donated poster board.
- **A second community connection worth one email:** any adult who hires teenagers, asked "what do you actually look for on a first resume?" Quote the answer on the Lesson 7.1 slide. It takes one email and it makes the whole lesson land differently.
- **The July email.** The most valuable community connection in this unit is the one that happens after it. A student who applies for a summer job in July will have lost the folder. A teacher who can email that student a scan of their own resume in July is doing something almost nobody does, and it takes four minutes of photographing on day 180 to make it possible.

## 13. Vocabulary for translation

See `Vocabulary 07.md`: 45 Tier 3 words with a one-line student definition and columns for Turkish, Portuguese, and Spanish, to be checked by a native speaker, plus sentence starters in all four languages for the three-part answer, a STAR story, giving interview feedback, and the six-part pitch.

Two notes specific to this unit's vocabulary. First, the business terms are the ones most likely to differ by country: what one country calls margin, another calls the difference, and break-even has no single everyday word in every language. The English term stays on every card because students will see the English word on a real job application, a real resume template, and a real price sheet. Second, my original vocabulary cards for the interview lesson were already translated into Turkish, Portuguese, and Spanish for my students, and those words are folded into this file.
