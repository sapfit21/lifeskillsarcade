# The project sheet (student version)

Name: ______________________ Date: __________ Station: ______ Idea path: ______________________

## The job

Find a used textile that was headed for the trash. Turn it into something a real person will actually use. Build it by hand in two class periods, using at least two of the four stitches you already know. Then explain it in 45 seconds to people who were not there while you made it.

You are not making a craft to take home and put in a drawer. You are making something with a user. The rubric asks who that person is, and "me" counts only if you can say what you will use it for.

## The four constraints

Every project, every path, no exceptions.

1. **It must start from a used textile.** Something somebody already owned: a t-shirt, jeans, a towel, a pillowcase, a sweater, a sheet, fabric scraps. Not new fabric off a roll.
2. **It must be finished in two build days.** Lesson 5.17, two periods of 40 minutes. Your plan has to fit that, and your reduced-scope version is your insurance.
3. **It must use at least two of the taught joins:** running stitch, backstitch, whipstitch, sewing on a button, or a machine seam reverse-locked at both ends. Fabric glue and hem tape can hold one join if you need them, but two joins have to be stitched, by hand or on the machine.
4. **It must be something somebody would actually use, and you have to name who.** A name, and what they will use it for.

No sewing machine is needed anywhere in this project; every path can be built by hand. Since the machine block in Topic 5.2, the machines are also open on both build days under the same rules, and a machine seam counts as a taught join. Your plan sheet says which joins you are using, hand or machine, before anything is cut.

## The ten example paths

Pick one, or design your own with my approval. The difficulty mark tells you how hard the construction is, not how good a grade it can earn. An Easy path built well scores higher than a Harder path built badly, every time.

## Where you get to choose

- **The path.** Ten examples plus design-your-own. Yours to pick. You may change paths up to the end of Lesson 5.16 with no penalty; after your plan is initialed and your fabric is cut, you switch to your reduced-scope version instead, which is also no penalty.
- **The material.** Anything in the scrap bin, anything the donation drive brings in, or something of your own with a signed note. Nothing in the bin is claimed until you pick it up.
- **The user.** Who the item is for is entirely yours: yourself with a stated use, a family member, a friend, a teacher, the classroom, a younger sibling, a pet.
- **The stitches.** Any two of the four, wherever they make sense. A third stitch is a stretch, not a requirement.
- **How you show it.** The 45 seconds can be given to a rotating audience, to one partner at your own table, or one-on-one to me. The display card can be handwritten, typed, or dictated to me word for word.
- **How much you add.** A reinforcement at a stress point, a second row of stitching, a hand-stitched detail, or a second small item if you finish early. Say what you added on the card and it counts on criterion 2.

## The sourcing plan, and the no-item-from-home path

Three ways to get a material, and nobody is stuck:

1. **The room's scrap bin.** Free, open from day 116, and stocked with more pieces than there are students. Anything in it is yours. **This is the default and it is a full-credit path.** A student who brings nothing from home builds exactly the same project and is scored on exactly the same rubric. Nobody is asked in front of the class whether they brought something.
2. **The class donation drive.** The bin by the door takes clean, used textiles from anybody, and everything that comes in goes into the scrap bin for the whole class. Contributing is not required and is not tracked.
3. **An item from home, with the signed permission note.** The note is the last page of `Handout 05.15`. Take it home, have the owner sign it, bring both. No signed note, no cutting, and the bin is right there.

Nothing valuable, nothing borrowed, nothing that belongs to somebody who has not signed. Everything clean and dry. If you bring something and then decide you do not want to cut it, that is fine and you use the bin.

## How it is scored

`Rubric 05 - Upcycle Challenge.md` has five criteria, each scored Not Yet (1), Approaching (2), Meets (3), or Exceeds (4), for 20 points:

1. The plan and the design brief
2. Construction quality, including the stitches used
3. The item is genuinely useful and finished
4. Tool safety and the station routine, across all four days
5. The display card and the explanation

You fill the self-assessment row first, with one line of evidence per criterion, before I score. The rubric goes out on day 136 with this sheet, so you build against it.

The project counts in the labs and projects category (40 percent of the marking period). Criterion 4 also feeds the participation and safety category. The Unit 5 Test on day 143 includes a repair decision and a sustainability item, so keep your reflection in mind.

# Teacher notes on the project

- This is the unit's synthesis project and it is deliberately smaller in scope than the Unit 4 room design. Unit 4 asked students to plan a space on paper; this one asks them to make a physical object that works, with their hands, in 80 minutes of build time. The whole design is built around that constraint: an approval gate before anyone cuts, a milestone at the end of each build day, a stuck protocol so one teacher can serve 21 makers, and a reduced-scope version written on day one so no student has to invent a fallback while panicking.
- The state's Sustainability theme module names this project almost exactly. Its Family and Consumer Sciences illustrative activity ends in "a recycle/upcycle/repurposing project, such as recycling t-shirts into reusable shopping bags," which is path 1 on the menu. The ATPD content module line 2. d), "Develop sustainability practices for repurposing apparel items," is the only place in the whole framework where the words repurposing apparel appear, and this project is the answer to it.
- The scrap bin is the single most important piece of preparation. Start it on day 116, the first day of the unit, with a sign. By day 136 it should hold more usable pieces than there are students. A bin with four pieces in it makes twenty students fight, and it makes the students with nothing at home feel it.
- Say the default out loud on day 136, before anyone asks: "Most people will use the bin. That is the normal way to do this project." That one sentence is why the no-item-from-home path works.
- The approval gate is not optional. Fabric shears live at the teacher's table until a plan is initialed. Five questions, sixty seconds, every student: used material, two taught stitches, cut size includes the seam allowance, order of work is possible, reduced scope written. That conversation prevents almost every failure this project can have.
- Scope is the enemy. The students who finish with nothing are the ones who planned a backpack, a quilt, or a stuffed animal. Constraint 2 and the reduced-scope line exist for them. Steer on day 136, not on day 139.
- Individual work with station partners is intentional. A group project on a sewing build produces one student sewing and three watching. Everyone makes their own item; the station is for help, tools, and peer review.
- Criterion 4 is scored from the clipboard across four days, not from one observation. Three needle and pin counts per day (one per station) are the evidence, and a student who runs a count correctly as Station Manager gets that noted. Rotate Station Manager daily so three different students run a count each day.
- Grade the item on what it is, not on how ambitious it was. A whipstitched phone sleeve with even stitches and a working fit scores higher than a half-built pencil case with a great idea. Say that on day 136 and students choose honestly.
- Photograph every item with its display card on day 141, no names and no faces. That photo set is the Unit 5 page on the website, the hallway display case, and next year's idea menu slides.
- **Material cost for the unit budget.** The textiles are free (scrap bin plus donation drive). The consumables this project adds beyond the Topic 5.2 sewing kit: cotton cord or shoelaces for drawstring bags (a spool, about $4), assorted buttons (a jar, about $6, or free from the drive), uncooked rice (a two-pound bag, about $2), fabric glue (one bottle, about $4), iron-on hem tape (one roll, about $4), binder clips (a box of 12, about $2), card stock for display cards (one pack, about $6, or the copier's heaviest paper for free), butcher paper or three cloths for the display tables (about $8 or free). Total new spend for the project, about $20 to $36 for a class of 21, or under $2 per student. Everything else (needles, pins, thread, shears, seam rippers, thimbles, threaders, chalk) is already in the Topic 5.2 line and is reused every year.
