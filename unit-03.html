1. Why this unit

I have watched what peer groups do to a middle school room. One group chat can change who sits where, who talks, and who stops raising a hand, and it can do it overnight. Kids do not learn from people they do not trust, and at 11 to 14 the people they trust most are each other. So this is the unit where the room learns to talk to each other: how to listen, how to say what you feel without starting a fight, how to say no to a friend and keep the friend, and how to tell a healthy relationship from one that is hurting you.

In my certification program I took Infant Development and Prenatal Care and Family Relations, and both changed how I teach this. Infant development gave me the egg under the napkin, the ages and stages chart, and a way to show a 12-year-old what a brain is doing at eight months and again at thirteen years. Family Relations gave me the language for what students already live: families that change shape, older siblings who are the after-school caregiver, and the plain fact that a family is where a kid first learns how to handle stress and conflict.

The unit ends with a job. Babysitting is the first paid work many of my students will do, and some of them are already doing it without pay for a younger brother or sister. They leave this unit knowing the seven rules of babysitting, safe sleep, what goes on the emergency card, why you never shake a baby, and how to build something for a child that fits the child's age, costs under $5, and passes a safety check twice. They test it, they present it, and they take it home. A student who can do that can be trusted with a kid, and that is the point.

2.3 Trends, issues, and news

  1. Phones and group chats. Most of the room is in at least one group chat, and that is where peer pressure now lives: the screenshot, the pile-on, the person left out of the chat. Lesson 3.6's ten scenario cards use everyday pressure (copying homework, skipping a class, sharing a photo, leaving someone out, a dare), Lesson 3.9's six scenarios are all online, and Lesson 3.13 has group chat rules.
  2. Screen time and sleep. Students know they are tired. Lesson 3.3 puts the sleep ranges from Lesson 2.6 on its slide, Lesson 3.8's sixth tool is sleep and food basics, and the Stress Plan in 3.9 requires one specific phone boundary (a time, a chat, a notification, a place) with a reason.
  3. Anxiety talk among teens. "I have anxiety" is a sentence students say to each other. Lesson 3.7 defines stress as a normal response with body signals on a blank outline, defines "mental health" in one line, and puts the who-to-ask list on the slide; anything that is more than stress goes to the counselor or the nurse, and 3.7, 3.9, and 3.12 say so.
  4. Changing family structures. Blended, single-parent, grandparent-led, two-household, adoptive, foster, and multigenerational families are all in the room. Lesson 3.14 puts every structure on one chart with the same four things every family does, so no student's family is the odd one, and its teacher notes say how the lesson handles students' own families.
  5. Older siblings as caregivers. Many students already pick up a younger sibling, feed them, and put them to bed. Lesson 3.15's home jobs list names younger siblings as a job, and Lessons 3.16 and 3.17 treat that as real caregiving, name the safety rules, and say where a family gets help when it is too much for a kid to carry alone (HDR 5. f)).
  6. Babysitting apps and age rules. The big babysitting apps require sitters to be 18, so a 12-year-old's first jobs come from neighbors and family. New York has no law that sets a minimum babysitting age; the American Red Cross babysitting course was written for ages 11 and up at last check. Lesson 3.17 puts the Red Cross course on one slide and Lesson 3.19's walk asks what a parent looks for.

2.4 What students this age find interesting

  1. Babies and little kids: what a baby can see, why a toddler drops the spoon fifty times, why an eight-month-old cries when a parent leaves the room.
  2. Whether what is happening to them is normal (it is), and hearing that from someone other than a parent.
  3. Friend drama, and a chance to act it out in a skit where nobody real gets hurt.
  4. A private map of what stresses them, that nobody collects.
  5. Getting paid to babysit, and knowing what to say to a parent to get the job.
  6. Building something with their hands, pricing it under $5, and watching a "kid" (a partner in role) try to break it.

3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

Big ideas:

  • People grow in four ways at once (physical, intellectual, emotional, social), at every stage, and what a person can do depends on where they are, not on how hard they try.
  • What you say to yourself and what your friends say to you both shape who you become. You can change both.
  • Stress is normal and has signals; a plan for it is a skill, not a personality trait.
  • Every relationship runs on the same things: listening, saying what you feel without attacking, respecting a boundary, and repairing after a fight.
  • A family is whoever does the four things a family does. Families change shape, and a change does not un-family the family.
  • Caring for a child is real work with real rules, and the rules are learnable at twelve.

Essential questions:

  1. How do people grow and change from birth to old age, and what is happening to me right now?
  2. Who decides who I am: my genes, my family, my friends, my phone, or me?
  3. What do I do when I am stressed, and what should I do?
  4. How do I say what I need and hear what someone else needs, even when we disagree?
  5. What makes a family a family, and what happens when a family changes?
  6. What does it take to be trusted with a child?

4. Knowledge and skills

Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):

  • The seven stages of the lifespan and the age ranges the lessons use: infancy (birth to 1), early childhood (1 to 5), middle childhood (6 to 11), adolescence (12 to 18), young adulthood (19 to 39), middle adulthood (40 to 64), older adulthood (65 and up); the ranges are approximate.
  • The four aspects of development (physical, intellectual, emotional, social) and how a change in one pulls on the others.
  • Six milestones from birth to five (rolling over, sitting, walking, first words, two-word sentences, pretend play); object permanence and why a baby under about eight months does not look for the hidden egg; separation anxiety as a sign the brain is working the way it should; what a 6-month-old, a 2-year-old, and a 4-year-old need and what safety step matches each new skill.
  • What changes at 11 to 14 in all four aspects; the myths and facts about adolescence; what current brain research says about the adolescent brain; the common problems of the stage.
  • Heredity versus environment, and the influences that shape a person (the private influences map).
  • Self-concept, self-image, self-talk; the factors that shape self-concept; the marks of a positive and a negative self-concept; what a filter does to a picture and what an unfiltered picture is for.
  • Peer, peer pressure, positive and negative pressure, direct and indirect pressure; the four refusal steps (say no clearly, give a reason, offer an alternative, walk away), used in any order and not all four at once; how family, peers, and media each push a decision.
  • Stress, stressor, the body signals of stress, "mental health" in one line; the six tools (breathing, walking, writing a list, music or movement, talking to someone, sleep and food basics) and a before and after rating for each; what a Stress Plan is (a stressor, two tools from the toolkit card, a person to talk to, a phone boundary).
  • Digital footprint; the four online channels; what lasts; the who-to-tell list for an online problem.
  • Hearing versus listening; the listening checklist; the I-message frame: I feel (feeling) when (what happened) because (why it matters) and I need (one specific thing).
  • Conflict, escalation, compromise, repair; the escalation ladder; the difference between prevent, manage, and repair; the repair steps; the coach checklist.
  • The signs of a healthy and an unhealthy relationship (the 24 cards); who to tell (the counselor, the dean, the nurse, the social worker, an adult at home).
  • What a boundary is and the boundary frame; the saying-no practice; the group chat rules; what friendship looks like at this age.
  • Family structures on one chart; the four things every family does (care, teach, provide, belong); the family life cycle; six transitions (a move, a new sibling, a job change or a job lost, a divorce or a separation, a death, a new country) and the rule that a transition changes the shape and does not un-family the family.
  • The home jobs list (meals, dishes, laundry, cleaning, trash, pets, younger siblings, errands, repairs, bills), which ones a 12 to 14 year old can do alone, and what a fair share is (not always equal); traditions and what they teach.
  • The eight jobs of a parent or caregiver (safety, food, health, love, teaching, limits, money, time); the 24-hour caregiver clock; what food, diapers, and child care cost in a month; readiness for parenting; the supports (the pediatric clinic, the family liaison, the public library, WIC, 211).
  • The seven rules of babysitting, including back to sleep in an empty crib and never shake a baby; the tube rule (anything that fits through a toilet paper tube can choke a child under 3); water and stairs as the two places you never look away; what goes on the emergency card; when to call 911 and what to say; the four job scenarios and which to say no to; the Red Cross course.
  • Eight human services and education careers (teacher, pediatric nurse, social worker, school counselor, child care director, speech therapist, coach, family lawyer), what each does, and the pathway line to each.

  • Test six stress tools in class, rate each before and after, and cut and keep a toolkit card.

  • Build the class conflict map; walk a scenario up and down the escalation ladder; in a triad, manage and then repair a conflict from a scenario card while a coach scores the checklist.
  • Sort 24 relationship cards on the three-column mat in a group of three and complete the answer sheet; name who to tell.
  • Say a boundary from the boundary frame and keep it when pushed once; complete the 90-second Communication Performance Task with a partner on the four-criterion checklist and self-score it.
  • Match twelve family scenario cards to the structures chart; place a transition on the life cycle chart and say what stays the same.
  • Build a fair-share chart for a fictional family card that assigns every home job and explains one assignment that is not equal but is fair; write the tradition card.

  • Plan, build, safety-check twice, test on a partner in role, change one thing, and demo a Babysitter's Activity Kit for one age (2 to 6) that builds one development area, costs under $5 from the bin price list, and comes with a parent card a parent can read in one minute.
  • Walk eight career cards with the "would I like this" check and write the pathway line to one career.

5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

  • Topic 3.1: Students will analyze how people change in the physical, intellectual, emotional, and social aspects across the seven stages of the lifespan, run and explain the object permanence demo, and evaluate the roles of heredity and environment in who they are becoming.
  • Topic 3.2: Students will evaluate the influence of self-talk, family, peers, and media on their own choices, perform a refusal skit that asserts a personal choice inside a peer group with the four refusal steps, and create a Stress Plan with two tested tools, a person to talk to, and a justified phone boundary.
  • Topic 3.3: Students will apply the listening checklist, the four-part I-message, and a stated boundary in a 90-second scenario with a partner, analyze a conflict on the escalation ladder and repair it in a role play, and justify which signs make a relationship healthy or unhealthy and who to tell.
  • Topic 3.4: Students will evaluate what every family does and what a transition changes, demonstrate the seven babysitting rules and the emergency card for a child in their care, and design, build, safety-check, test, and present a Babysitter's Activity Kit that fits one age, builds one development area, costs under $5, and passes the checklist twice.

8. Project

Name: Babysitter's Activity Kit and Family Showcase. Full sheet in Project 03 - Babysitters Activity Kit and Family Showcase.md (handed out at the start of Lesson 3.18 Day 1 with the rubric; Lesson 3.17's safety walk is the rehearsal for its safety checklist); rubric in Rubric 03 - Babysitters Activity Kit.md, five criteria at four levels, 20 points. The Project 03 sheet is the authority on the deliverables, the ages, the dollar cap, and the showcase format; this is the summary.

The scenario: You have been asked to babysit a child on Saturday afternoon, and the parent says the child gets bored fast. You show up with one activity you designed for exactly this child's age: safe enough that the parent does not have to worry, cheap enough that you made it for under $5, and good enough that the child learns something while playing. You pick the age (2, 3, 4, 5, or 6), read what a child that age is working on in each of the four areas on the Ages and Stages chart, and pick one area and one thing. Your kit builds that one thing, not four. You pick a kit type from the menu (a sensory bag, a story with props, a sorting game, a movement game, a simple craft), each with an age range and a watch-out line.

Criteria and constraints, as the sheet sets them: fit the age exactly; build one development area and say which and why; pass every line of the safety checklist, twice; cost under $5 from the posted bin price list; come with a parent card a parent can read in one minute. Bin materials only, no food in any kit, nothing sharp, nothing plugged in, nothing that goes in a mouth, built in one class period, and the tube rule: for ages 2 and 3, nothing loose that fits through a toilet paper tube; for ages 4 to 6, small pieces only if the card says "with an adult."

Deliverables: (1) the plan template, signed by the teacher on Day 1, with the age, the area, the one thing, the kit type, the three steps, the priced materials list under $5, the first safety check, any change made during the build, and the test notes; (2) the ten-line safety checklist, initialed by the student and by a partner; (3) the parent card: the kit's name, the age, what it builds in one sentence, three steps in plain language, one safety line, one cleanup line, and "made by" with first name or initials only; (4) the kit itself, complete and labeled; (5) the two-minute demo at the Family Showcase. Also turned in, not scored: the self-assessment row and the gallery card (one kit you would bring to a job and why).

Steps by day: Day 1 (Lesson 3.18, day 87): the brief, the menu, the criteria and constraints; pick an age and an area; plan on the template; price the materials; first safety check; the teacher signs the plan. Day 2 (day 88): build from the bin; write any change on the plan; write the parent card; run the checklist and have a partner run it again; fix what fails. Day 3 (day 89): test with a partner playing the child's age; write the test notes and make one change; demo at the Family Showcase; self-assess; submit everything together. A student absent on Day 2 or Day 3 does the written alternative on the sheet (a full plan, a parent card, and a written test) scored on the same rubric.

The Family Showcase: the Day 3 demo round, not a separate event. Kits sit on the counter under age signs (2, 3, 4, 5, 6) with the parent card in front of each, the tradition cards from Lesson 3.15 are posted on the wall above (name optional), each student or pair gives a two-minute demo at their kit, and everyone else walks the showcase with a gallery card. If the room has a hallway case, the kits and cards stay up for the rest of the unit.

Group or individual: alone or in a pair, the student's choice. A pair makes one kit and each person gives half the demo; the pair gets one score on criteria 1 to 4 and each person gets their own score on criterion 5.

Choice built in: the age (any of five); the area and the one thing; the kit type (any of five that fits the age); alone or with a partner; the parent card written, drawn as a three-box picture card, both, or in two languages; the demo at the kit for the class or at the table for the teacher only.

ELL and IEP supports, as the sheet lists them: sentence starters for the parent card and the demo in Spanish, to be verified by a native speaker (the sheet's own set; Vocabulary 03.md adds a few more); the picture instruction card with a safety box; the plan template with checkboxes and the bin prices printed so only the quantity is written; kit types with large pieces (the movement game, the story with props) for a student who cannot work with small pieces; extended time (the plan into Day 2, the card into Day 3); the demo for the teacher only; the written alternative for an absence.

How it is presented and scored: the student fills the self-assessment row first with one sentence of evidence per criterion, then the teacher scores. A kit that fails a safety line at the showcase (a loose piece that fits through the tube on a kit for a 2 or 3 year old) is capped at Approaching on criterion 2 until it is fixed, and it can be fixed the same day. A late project is accepted with a one-level drop on one criterion, never a zero. The grade is the rubric, not the applause: a plain sorting game that fits a 4-year-old, costs $1.40 from the list, passes the checklist twice, and comes with a clear card scores higher than a beautiful kit with a small part in it. I say that on Day 1 and again before the showcase. Kits may be donated after the showcase (a pre-K, an after-school program, a library children's room, a family shelter's play area) with permission and a second safety look by that adult, only kits that passed every line, and never with a student's last name on the card.

11. Health and other-department coordination

Health: the health course owns puberty and the body changes of adolescence, body image and eating concerns, reproductive health, substance use, mental health diagnoses, and, in some buildings, hands-only CPR. FACS stops at the line every time and says so on the slide. Lesson 3.3 teaches adolescence as a stage in the four aspects, sorts myths from facts, uses the sleep ranges from Lesson 2.6, and marks the body changes as the health teacher's; nothing in it asks a student to say anything about their own body out loud. Lesson 3.5 stays on self-concept and self-talk and never edits a picture of a person (the filtered pair is an object). Lesson 3.6's coordination line keeps substances and sexual pressure off the scenario cards; if a student brings one up, the four refusal steps still apply and the conversation goes to health, and if the health teacher wants one shared scenario, it runs in both rooms with the same four steps in the same order. Lessons 3.7 and 3.8 teach stress as a normal response with body signals and six tools, define "mental health" in one line, and send anything beyond stress to the who-to-ask list. Lesson 3.12 teaches the signs of a healthy and an unhealthy relationship for friendships and family; the health teacher owns consent and dating violence in the grade 8 curriculum and should know that 3.12 and 3.13 run in mid-January. Lesson 3.17 covers the choking rule and the emergency card but not CPR; if health teaches hands-only CPR, 3.17 says so and does not repeat it.

The school counselor: the counselor is on the who-to-ask slide in Lesson 3.7 and the who-to-tell slide in 3.9, visits for the last ten minutes of Lesson 3.12 (day 80) to say where the office is, what happens when a student comes in (it is not trouble), and take two questions, and receives the same-day referral for anything written on an exit strip, a Stress Plan, or a family line that describes harm. The counselor also gets a copy of the Stress Plan mini rubric so the language matches what the office uses.

The school nurse: Lesson 3.17's connections line offers the nurse Step 1's safe sleep demonstration (the shoebox crib) to say in the nurse's own words, and the nurse is on the 3.7 who-to-ask list. If the nurse cannot come, the lesson runs from the slides and the shoebox; either way the nurse should read the seven rules and the emergency card before they print.

Science: the science course reaches the brain and the nervous system in grade 7 or 8. Lessons 3.2 and 3.3 use two facts (an infant brain builds connections fast, which is why object permanence and separation anxiety show up when they do; current brain research on the adolescent brain) and name the science teacher as the source. ELA: the I-message frame is a sentence frame; the parent card is writing for an adult audience in three plain steps; the refusal skit is dialogue. Share the I-message frame and the parent card template with the ELA teacher so students see one structure in two rooms.

Social studies: family structures across cultures and the tradition card (3.14 and 3.15) can borrow whatever the social studies course has on family and community; FACS stays on what a family does and what a transition changes.

Art: the kit uses color, size, and texture chosen for one age, and the picture version of the parent card draws three steps with one word each; the art room can lend construction paper, yarn, and craft sticks to the bin.

The scenario

You have been asked to babysit a child on Saturday afternoon. The parent says the child gets bored fast. You are going to show up with something in your bag that no other babysitter brings: one activity you designed for exactly this child's age, safe enough that the parent does not have to worry, cheap enough that you made it for under $5, and good enough that the child learns something while playing.

That is the kit. You will plan it, build it, write a card so the parent knows how to use it, check it for safety twice, test it with a partner playing the child, and show it at the Family Showcase.

Step 1: Pick your child

Choose one age: 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6. The ages-and-stages chart on Handout 03.18 tells you what a child that age is working on in each area:

  • Physical: big movements (run, hop, climb) and small movements (pinch, stack, draw)

  • Emotional: naming feelings, handling "no," waiting

Pick one area and one thing the child is working on. Your kit builds that one thing. Not four things. One.

403.4responsibilityA job that is yours to do, that others count on you for.responsabilidad

423.4parentingThe work of raising a child: keeping them safe, fed, loved, and taught, every day.crianza (paternidad y maternidad)
433.4readinessBeing ready for something big, like parenting, in body, in friendships, in feelings, and in money.preparación (estar listo)
443.4safe sleepThe rules for putting a baby to sleep: alone, on the back, in a crib, with nothing else in it.sueño seguro
453.4supervisionWatching a child closely enough to stop harm before it happens. How close depends on the age.supervisión
463.4emergencyA situation where someone could be badly hurt and you need help now. Call 911 and say where you are.emergencia
473.4human servicesJobs that help people and families directly: social worker, counselor, child care teacher, family support worker.servicios humanos (servicios sociales)
