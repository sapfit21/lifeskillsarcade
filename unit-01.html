1. Why this unit (rationale, in Sal's voice)

I put the kitchen first because the first cooking lab is what wins the class. At Brentwood I ran three or four kitchens at once with role cards at every station and a zero-knife policy, and the day students pulled their first mug cake out of the microwave was the day the room became ours. Nothing else I teach gets that reaction. But nobody cooks in my room until they pass the safety exam, so this unit opens with hazards, handwashing, the danger zone, and a class contract the students write themselves.

The second half of the unit is where my business background does the work. Students learn to read a Nutrition Facts label the way I learned to read a P&L: what is the serving, what is the real number, what is the company hoping you skip. Then they plan a dinner for a family of four, price it from a real store circular, check the math with a partner, and cook one dish from their plan. I ran Feed a Family of Four on $40 and the King Kullen shopping project at Brentwood; both are in here, rewritten for 40 minutes and grades 6 to 8.

At 11 to 14, most of my students already make some of their own food and some of them shop for their family. This unit takes what they are already doing and makes them better at it: safer, more accurate, more skeptical of what they see online, and able to feed four people well for less money than they thought.

2.2 How the CTE themes come in

  • Health, Safety, and Wellness (primary): every lab day runs on the safety rules students wrote in Lesson 1.4, and the nutrition topic is built on HSW 1. Health Practices and HSW 2. Disease Prevention.
  • Financial and Consumer Literacy: the $40 project, the circular, unit price, and store brands make students do real consumer math on a real budget.
  • Problem Solving and Innovation: the class solves the safety contract as a problem (the state's own illustrative activity for this module) and evaluates food videos as sources in Lesson 1.12.
  • Communication and Interpersonal Relationships: role cards, the peer math check, the tasting gallery, and the pitch all require students to work in a group to get one thing done.
  • Sustainability: Lesson 1.17 covers leftovers, FIFO, and lab waste, and every lab ends with a waste count at the station.
  • Career and Community Opportunities: Lesson 1.22 puts dietitian, chef, food safety inspector, and school food service on the table, with the pathway to each.

2.3 Trends, issues, and news

  1. Grocery prices. Students hear adults talk about the cost of eggs, milk, and cereal. The $40 project makes the number real.
  2. Viral food videos. Recipe hacks, "what I eat in a day," and supplement pitches reach students on their phones before any adult explains what a credible source is. Lesson 1.12 gives them a test to run.
  3. Ultra-processed foods and energy drinks. Both are in the news and in their backpacks. The label lessons let students read the sodium, added sugar, and caffeine themselves instead of being told.
  4. Air fryers and small appliances. Many families cook with an air fryer, a microwave, and a rice cooker more than a stove. Lesson 1.5 treats these as real tools, not novelties.
  5. Allergen labeling. The nine major allergens must be named on packaged food. Students learn where to find them because someone in every class has an allergy, and because it feeds the project's family scenario.

2.4 What students this age find interesting

  1. Cooking something they can eat in the same period.
  2. Competing as a team against a clock (the Hazard Hunt, the Silent Measurement Challenge).
  3. Being the one who catches the trick (a label with a serving size of half a bag, a video that is selling something).
  4. Being trusted with real money math and a real store flyer.
  5. Feeding people they care about, and being told their plan would work.

2.5 Links to other subjects

  • Math: fractions and equivalents in measuring (Lesson 1.6), doubling and halving a recipe (1.7), unit price as division and comparison (1.16), and the $40 budget as an addition and estimation problem (1.18, 1.19).
  • Science: heat transfer and why a pot handle burns (1.3), bacteria growth in the danger zone (1.2), leavening in the mug cake (1.8), and the Maillard browning on the nachos (1.13).
  • ELA: a recipe as procedural text (1.7), the label as an informational text (1.11), source evaluation (1.12), and the pitch as a short persuasive speech (1.21).
  • Health: nutrients and MyPlate are pre-assessed and coordinated so FACS time goes to labels, portions on a real plate, cooking, and shopping (see Section 11).
  • Art: the menu card in Lesson 1.21 uses layout, color, and one drawn or cut image to sell the meal.
  • Social studies (light): the tasting gallery in 1.21 asks students to share a dish and a food custom from home.

3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

Big ideas:

  • A kitchen is only safe when everyone in it can see the hazard before it becomes an accident. Safety is a habit, not a poster.
  • Measuring, reading, and following a recipe are the skills that let a person cook anything, not just today's dish.
  • A food label is written by the company that sells the food. Reading it is a skill; believing it is a choice.
  • Feeding people well is a planning problem and a money problem before it is a cooking problem.

Essential questions:

  1. What could go wrong in this kitchen today, and what stops it?
  2. How do I know a recipe will come out the way it should?
  3. What is actually in this food, and how do I know who to trust about it?
  4. How do I feed four people a good dinner on $40?
  5. Who does this work for a living, and could I?

4. Knowledge and skills

Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):

  • The six hazard families (burns, cuts, falls, fire, electrical, food contamination) and one prevention step for each.
  • The handwashing steps; the temperature danger zone (40 F to 140 F) and the two-hour rule; what cross contamination is and how it travels.
  • What to do for a grease fire, a burn, a cut, and a choking person; where the first aid kit, fire extinguisher, and Safety Data Sheets are.
  • The name and use of about 20 common kitchen tools, including the microwave, oven, and the small appliances in this room.
  • Measuring equivalents: 3 tsp in 1 Tbsp, 4 Tbsp in 1/4 cup, 16 Tbsp in 1 cup, 8 fl oz in 1 cup, 2 cups in 1 pint, 4 cups in 1 quart.
  • Recipe parts: title, yield, ingredients, equipment, steps, time; the standard abbreviations.
  • The six nutrients (carbohydrate, protein, fat, vitamins, minerals, water), a food source for each, and one job each does.
  • MyPlate's five groups and what a balanced plate looks like on a real plate at a real meal.
  • The parts of a Nutrition Facts label: serving size, servings per container, calories, percent daily value, sodium, fiber, added sugar, and the allergen line.
  • What makes a nutrition source credible (who wrote it, what they are selling, whether it agrees with a known source).
  • Unit price, store brand versus name brand, and how a store circular is organized.
  • Safe storage: refrigerator and freezer temperatures, FIFO, "use by" versus "best by," leftovers within two hours, and reheating.
  • Four careers in nutrition and culinary arts and the education each one takes.

Skills (what students will be able to do):

  • Find and rank hazards in a kitchen and write a rule that prevents each one.
  • Wash hands correctly, use a food thermometer, and keep raw and ready-to-eat foods apart.
  • Respond to a small kitchen emergency with the right first step.
  • Select the right tool for a task and use it safely.
  • Measure dry, liquid, and small quantities accurately and level them off.
  • Read a recipe, put the steps in order, and double or halve it.
  • Prepare a simple recipe in a team using role cards and a station flowchart.
  • Build a balanced plate from food cards and from a real meal.
  • Read a label to find serving size, calories, sodium, added sugar, fiber, and allergens, and compare two products.
  • Test a food claim against a credible source and say whether to believe it.
  • Plan a dinner for four that meets MyPlate, fits the family's needs and likes, and costs $40 or less.
  • Write and organize a shopping list from a circular; compute unit price and choose the better buy.
  • Store food and leftovers safely and cut lab waste.
  • Cook and serve one dish from the plan, set a place, and share food with respect for other people's customs.
  • Pitch the meal in 60 seconds with a menu card.

5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

  • Topic 1.1: Students will evaluate the hazards in a working kitchen and create a class Kitchen Safety Contract whose rules prevent them, then pass a safety exam that shows they can apply the contract before they cook.
  • Topic 1.2: Students will analyze a simple recipe for tools, measurements, and steps, scale it up or down correctly, and prepare it in a team with every measurement accurate and every safety rule followed.
  • Topic 1.3: Students will analyze Nutrition Facts labels and online food claims against MyPlate and a credible source, and justify which of two foods is the better choice for a stated need.
  • Topic 1.4: Students will design a dinner for a family of four that meets MyPlate, fits the family's needs and likes, and costs $40 or less from a real circular; cook one dish from it; and defend the plan in a 60-second pitch.

8. Project

Name: Feed a Family of Four on $40. Full sheet in Project 01 - Feed a Family of Four on 40 Dollars.md; rubric in Rubric 01 - Feed a Family of Four.md.

The scenario: A family of four (two adults, a 12-year-old, and a 6-year-old) has $40 for one dinner and the leftovers. One member has a stated need drawn from a card (a nut allergy, a vegetarian, an athlete who needs protein, a grandparent who needs low sodium, a picky six-year-old). The team's job is to plan a dinner that meets MyPlate, fits the need, feeds four, and costs $40 or less priced from this week's circular, then cook one dish from it and sell the plan to the class.

Deliverables: (1) a meal plan sheet with the dish list and a MyPlate check; (2) a shopping list organized by store section with prices from the circular, unit price on at least two items, and a total at or under $40; (3) one dish from the plan cooked in the Budget Meal Lab and scored on the lab rubric; (4) a menu card; (5) a 60-second pitch.

Steps by day: Day 1 (Lesson 1.18, day 1): the $12 versus $38 dinner hook, the scenario and need cards, teams pick a main dish. Day 2 (1.18, day 2): plan the plate, build the list from the circular, price it. Day 3 (1.19): swap plans with another team for a peer math check and a MyPlate check, then fix what they found. Days 4 and 5 (1.20): cook one dish, set a place, serve it, clean, store the leftovers. Day 6 (1.21): menu card, pitch, tasting gallery, class vote.

Group or individual: teams of three or four (the same teams as the lab stations). Each student owns one deliverable and signs it; the plan and the pitch are shared.

Choice built in: the main dish (from a list of six that fit the zero-knife, pre-cooked-meat rules, or a family dish the team proposes and I approve), the need card, the store (any circular I bring in or one the family uses), and the form of the menu card (drawn, cut paper, or a one-page digital layout).

ELL and IEP supports: sentence starters for the pitch in Turkish, Portuguese, and Spanish (Vocabulary 01.md); a picture-based circular for the shopping list; the price math as a fill-in table with the operations shown; a role on the team that fits the student (the recorder, the price checker, the presenter, the plating lead); a pitch that can be read from the card or delivered by two students together; extra time on Day 3.

How it is presented: each team pitches for 60 seconds at the tasting gallery while the class tastes the dish, then the class votes for the meal it would eat at home. Menu cards go up on the wall for the rest of the marking period. [Sal: your Brentwood run had a pitch and jingle and a class vote; keep the jingle if it still works for you.]

The scenario

A family of four has $40 for tonight's dinner. That is $10 a person. They want to eat well. They have a few rules (your family card tells you what they are), a pantry with a few things already in it, and about 45 minutes to cook.

Your team is the family's meal planner. You will plan the dinner, price it to the penny from this week's store circular, check it against MyPlate, cook one dish from it in our kitchen, make a menu card, and pitch it to the class in sixty seconds. The class votes on which team's dinner best fits its family and its budget.

The two budgets

  • $40 is the family's dinner. Four full servings, everything on the plate, priced from the circular. You pay for the whole package, not the spoonful you use. Pantry items on your family card cost $0.
  • $20 is the lab budget. That is what I can spend so your team can cook one dish from your plan for seven tasting portions in our kitchen. Your station card has to come in at $20 or under.

The room's rules for the cooked dish

  • No knives. Anything that needs cutting comes pre-cut, pre-shredded, or gets torn or shredded with two forks.
  • Pre-cooked meat only (deli rotisserie chicken, frozen cooked meatballs, canned beans if no meat). You reheat it to 165 F. You do not cook raw meat.
  • Stovetop and oven only. No frying.
  • I handle the oven door and I drain the pasta. You call me.
  • Twenty minutes of cooking time. If it takes longer, it is the wrong dish for the lab (it can still be on your $40 plan).
