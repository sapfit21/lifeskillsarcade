1. Why this unit

I took a graduate course called The Meanings of Dress, and the thing I wrote about was dress as communication and identity: that clothing says something to other people whether or not you meant it to. Then Spirit Week happened. After 80s Day the hallway comments got mean, and a colleague and I stopped the lesson we had planned and built a class segment on what is and is not okay to say about what somebody is wearing. That segment is Lesson 5.2 in this unit, and it is the reason the unit opens with what clothing communicates instead of with a needle.

The consumer half of this unit I can teach cold. I have a business background, I priced a whole cooking project off a real supermarket circular with my students, and cost per wear is the same move as unit price: divide the money by the thing you actually get. Lesson 5.13 is division, and Lesson 5.12 is a hoodie on a table with four price tags and four vendor types, and those two days are the ones I would show a principal first.

Here is what I am honest about. I had not taught sewing before I built this unit. I observed a colleague's sewing labs and I analyzed one of them for a course, so I have watched a room of twelve-year-olds hold needles, but I had never run it myself. So the hand sewing progression in Topic 5.2 is written step by step, one skill a day, with a demonstration rig for the back row, the six needle rules, and a count-out and count-in routine that runs every single day tools are out. It is written that way so it can be taught from the page, and taught well, by me in my first year of it and by whoever follows me.

The claim the unit makes is small and it is specific. A student who leaves this class can sew a button back on, fix a hem, judge whether a garment is worth the money, and say what a piece of clothing communicates without insulting anybody. Four things. Every one of them is checkable, and every one of them is useful the week after the unit ends.

2.3 Trends, issues, and news

  1. Fast fashion and haul culture. Students watch people open bags of clothes on a screen. Lesson 5.14 takes that apart with four features (speed, volume, price, thin construction), states the costs that are not on the tag in two sentences each, and says the other side out loud: cheap clothing is real help to a family with little money. The sentence that keeps it working is "I am not telling you where to shop."
  2. Resale and thrift apps. Buying and selling used clothing on a phone is normal now. Lesson 5.12 makes the online marketplace one of the four vendor types and builds the landed cost off a real packing slip, shipping and returns included.
  3. Sneaker and clothing resale as a teen business. Some students already buy to flip. Lesson 5.13's cost per wear and Lesson 5.12's quality checks are exactly the tools a reseller uses, and Lesson 5.19 names the two jobs on the list that are commonly run as a small business.
  4. Repair culture and visible mending. Mending as something to show rather than hide is a current thing, and Lesson 5.9's patch station offers the visible mend in bright floss as one of two real options.
  5. Dress codes and school policy debates. Students argue about this already. Lesson 5.1 analyzes a real workplace dress code rule for what it protects and what it costs the worker, and the school's own code gets quoted exactly, not paraphrased.
  6. Microplastics from synthetic fabric. Synthetic fleece sheds fiber in the wash. Lesson 5.3 identifies polyester and nylon by test, and Lesson 5.14 names the microfiber shed as one of the three costs that are not on the tag, with its source and year on the slide.

2.4 What students this age find interesting

  1. Making a real object with their own hands that somebody else will use.
  2. Being trusted with a sharp tool, on a routine, with a job title attached (the Station Manager runs the count).
  3. Money, and finding out that the best made sweatshirt on the table was also the cheapest.
  4. Their own clothes, their own style, and the question of who gets to comment on it.
  5. Destroying something on purpose in front of the class: the pull test on two seams, the sweater ruined in a hot wash, the three snips of fiber over a pie pan.

2.5 Links to other subjects

  • Math: cost per wear is division with a unit written on the answer (5.13, 5.14). Measuring to the half inch and the quarter inch, seam allowance, finished size against cut size, and perimeter are the whole of Lessons 5.10 and 5.16. Stitches per inch is counting a rate. The landed cost in Lesson 5.12 is a total with shipping and tax. The repair-or-replace sheet in Lesson 5.9 is a break-even comparison.
  • Science: fibers are materials, and Lesson 5.3 tests six of them for absorbency, stretch, and wicking, then burns three (cotton burns and leaves crushable ash, wool curls and smells like hair, polyester melts and leaves a hard bead) to make one safety point about melting near heat. Polyester and nylon are polymers, which the science teacher may already have named. Lesson 5.4's stain station is applied chemistry: a base for grease, an acid for the juice, a solvent for the ink, one variable per cup.
  • ELA: the display card and the 45-second explanation in Lesson 5.18 are a short informative text and a short informative presentation with a required structure. The fact table in Lesson 5.14 is nonfiction reading: who published it, what year, what does it count, what does it not count. Two stars and a step is a peer feedback protocol the ELA teacher probably already uses.
  • Social studies: where clothes are made, who makes them, and what a country's garment industry is worth to it. Lesson 5.14's journey of one sweatshirt ends in a bale sold to a used-clothing dealer for export, which is global trade from the plate side.
  • Art: color, texture, and pattern are the design vocabulary students already met in Unit 4's Color in the Home work, used again here on fabric. Lesson 5.16 cites National FCS 16.3.3 on the elements and principles of design in constructing and altering apparel, and Lesson 5.18's three display tables are composition.

3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

Big ideas:

  • Clothing communicates whether or not you meant it to, and what it says depends on the setting, the audience, and who is looking.
  • What a garment is made of decides how it behaves: how it feels, whether it stretches, whether it dries, how it must be washed, and how it fails.
  • Most clothes are thrown away long before they stop working. Four skills, a hem, a seam, a patch, and a button, are the difference between a garment being finished and being fixable.
  • The price on the tag is not the cost. The cost is the price divided by the number of times you actually wear it, plus what you cannot see: shipping, returns, water, labor, and where it goes when you are done.
  • A tool that can hurt somebody gets a routine, not a warning. The count-out and count-in is how a room of twenty-one people uses needles safely every day.
  • Making something takes a plan with measurements on it, and the plan gets better when somebody makes you defend it before you cut.

Essential questions:

  1. What does what I am wearing say about me, and who gets to decide what it says?
  2. How do I know what a piece of clothing is made of, and what does that tell me about how to keep it alive?
  3. Is this worth fixing, and how do I know?
  4. What does this shirt actually cost me, and what does it cost that is not on the receipt?
  5. What can I make out of something somebody else threw away?
  6. Who does this for a living, and what would it take for me to be one of them?

4. Knowledge and skills

Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):

  • The four jobs clothing does: protect, fit the activity, fit the setting, say something. Personal protective equipment, dress code, uniform, closed-toe shoe, layer.
  • Clothing as nonverbal communication: first impression, personal style, cultural dress, generation, intent against impact.
  • Natural fibers (cotton, wool, silk, linen) and their sources; manufactured fibers (polyester, nylon, rayon, spandex); blend; what each one is good and bad at; wicking, absorbent, breathable, static.
  • What a burn test shows: cotton burns to crushable ash, wool curls and self-extinguishes, polyester melts to a hard bead, and a fiber that melts is dangerous near heat.
  • The five care symbol families (wash, bleach, dry, iron, professional care) and what dots, bars, and X marks mean. Care labels are required by law, which is why the tag is there.
  • The laundry sequence from sort to fold, the three care groups, and what ruins clothes: hot water on wool, high heat on spandex, bleach on color, a stain that was never pretreated.
  • Every tool in the station kit by name and job, and where each one lives when it is not in a hand. Six needle rules. The six steps of the count-out and count-in.
  • Four stitches and what each is for: running stitch (basting, gathering, a channel), backstitch (a seam that carries weight), whipstitch (joining two edges, finishing a raw edge), the button stitch with a shank. Blanket stitch on the no-machine fallback path.
  • Seam, seam line, seam allowance, right side and wrong side, hem, patch, visible mending, shank, pattern, mock-up.
  • Seven parts of a sewing machine, the four machine safety rules, the machine close-down routine, the broken needle rule, and the machine doctor tag. The threading path, top and bobbin.
  • Four vendor types and the cost advantage and risk of each. Five hidden costs of buying online. Four quality checks you can run in a store in under a minute, and the five-point inspection: fiber content, seam type, stitches per inch, reinforcement at stress points, buttons and closures.
  • Cost per wear as a unit rate. Upcycle against recycle against repurpose. Fast fashion in four features. Where a donated garment actually goes: sorting center, resale floor, bale for export, wiping rags, shredded fiber, landfill.
  • Thirteen textile and apparel careers, three education paths, the two that are commonly small businesses, and what a five-repair portfolio is.

Skills (what students will be able to do):

  • Select clothing and footwear for five situations and justify each by weather, activity, and setting, and evaluate one real workplace dress code rule for what it protects and what it costs.
  • Analyze how the same shirt reads differently in three settings, and write and defend one line of a class agreement about commenting on what people wear.
  • Test six unlabeled swatches by feel, stretch, and water drop, record what each does, and choose the best fiber for a stated use using two performance characteristics from their own data.
  • Read a care label, sort a basket of real garments into three care groups, state the laundry sequence in order, and name the step where a ruined garment went wrong.
  • Compare three treatments on one stain and compute the cost of the treatment against the cost of replacing the garment.
  • Name ten hand sewing tools and their storage places, state the six needle rules, and run a count-out and count-in with a matching number as Station Manager.
  • Thread a needle, tie a knot that holds, and sew a running stitch, a backstitch, a whipstitch, and a sewn-on button with a shank that holds a firm tug, then judge each against even length, straight line, and no puckering.
  • Diagnose three kinds of garment damage and complete at least two of a hem, a seam repair, and a patch with the correct stitch for each.
  • Decide repair or replace with the five-step decision model and defend it with the numbers.
  • Measure, mark, and cut from a paper pattern to within a quarter inch, pin, construct a drawstring bag on the machine (or a felt pouch by hand), and evaluate it against a five-point checklist.
  • Identify seven machine parts and four machine rules, thread the top and bobbin verified by a buddy, drive a straight line, a curve, and a pivoted square on paper, and sew a straight machine seam reverse-locked at both ends.
  • Assemble a six-part stitch sampler, hand and machine, and self-score it before it is scored.
  • Run four quality checks on a garment in hand, including counting stitches per inch, and recommend one item for a stated buyer against a second option using price, quality, and one hidden cost.
  • Compute cost per wear, rank four garments from most to least likely to last with a reason for each rank, and build a three-option comparison for a real clothing decision of their own.
  • Read a statistic for its source, its year, what it counts, and what it does not.
  • Write a design brief with a used starting material, a named user, two stitches, an order of work, the one thing most likely to go wrong, and a reduced-scope version, then plan it with four or more measurements, prototype the hardest part, and revise from what the mock-up showed.
  • Build the planned item by hand in two periods using the stuck protocol, self-check it against a seven-line finishing checklist, and fix one problem they found themselves.
  • Write a four-field display card, deliver a four-part 45-second explanation pointing at the item, give two-stars-and-a-step feedback that names a specific feature, and evaluate their own use of the design process.
  • Place thirteen careers on three education paths, complete the fit self-check honestly, and start a five-repair portfolio with this project as entry one.

5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

  • Topic 5.1: Students will analyze what clothing does and says by matching clothing to five situations, comparing one garment across three settings, and testing six fibers, then evaluate a care label and a stain treatment against the cost of replacing the garment.
  • Topic 5.2: Students will demonstrate and evaluate four hand sewing techniques and a full station safety routine by producing a six-part stitch sampler, two completed repairs, and one finished hand-sewn item judged against a stitch quality checklist.
  • Topic 5.3: Students will evaluate clothing purchases by comparing the same item across four vendor types, computing cost per wear, inspecting four garments for construction quality, and recommending one option for a named buyer with a defense that uses price, quality, and one cost that is not on the tag.
  • Topic 5.4: Students will design and create a useful object from a used textile by writing a design brief against four constraints, prototyping the hardest part, building it by hand in two periods with two of the four taught stitches, and evaluating both the finished item and their own use of the design process.
  • Topic 5.5: Students will evaluate thirteen textile and apparel careers against their own interests and the training each requires, and justify one first step a middle school student could actually take.

A student who can control a hand needle has already learned the only thing a machine cannot teach: patience with fabric.

8. Project

Name: Upcycle Challenge. Full sheet in Project 05 - Upcycle Challenge.md (handed out in Lesson 5.15 with the rubric); scored on Rubric 05 - Upcycle Challenge.md.

The scenario: find a used textile that was headed for the trash, turn it into something a real person will actually use, build it by hand in two class periods with at least two of the four stitches the class already knows, and explain it in 45 seconds to people who were not there while it was made. Individual work at a station of about seven, with a station partner for help and peer review. Six days, days 136 to 141.

The four constraints, every path, no exceptions: it starts from a used textile, not new fabric off a roll; it is finished in two build days; it uses at least two of the taught joins, and since D19 a machine seam counts as one of them (fabric glue or hem tape can hold one join, two joins are stitched, by hand or machine); and it is something somebody would actually use, with that person named. The machines are available on both build days for any student whose plan calls for them, with the same rules as Topic 5.2; no plan is required to use one.

Deliverables (eight, all named in Project 05): (1) the design brief, with the starting material's size and condition, the finished item, the named user, the two stitches, three steps in order, the likeliest failure, and the reduced-scope version; (2) the plan sheet with four or more measurements, finished size and cut size with the seam allowance, the three marks, the numbered order of work, the mock-up of the tricky part, what the mock-up changed, the peer review, and my initials; (3) the finished item; (4) tool safety and the station routine across all four days tools are out; (5) the four-field display card, name on the back only; (6) the four-part 45-second explanation; (7) the five-prompt reflection; (8) the self-assessment row of Rubric 05, filled before I score.

Steps by day: Lesson 5.15 (day 136) upcycle against recycle, the ten paths, the four constraints, pick a material, write the brief, rubric goes out. Lesson 5.16 (day 137) plan sheet, mock-up, the approval conversation at my table (nothing is cut before I initial), peer review. Lesson 5.17 Day 1 (day 138) count-out, cut, mark, pin, first full seam, stuck protocol, milestone check, count-in. Lesson 5.17 Day 2 (day 139) finish, reduced-scope call at minute 10 if behind, the seven-line finishing checklist self-checked and initialed. Lesson 5.18 Day 1 (day 140) ten-minute finishing window, display card, the 45-second planner, gallery set up on three tables, two timed practice rounds. Lesson 5.18 Day 2 (day 141) gallery walk with two feedback slips minimum, explanations in rotation, the three-category vote, the reflection, the folder in.

Group or individual: individual, at a station of about seven. The only role is Station Manager, and it rotates every sewing day so three different students run a count.

Choice built in: the path (ten examples plus design your own), the material (scrap bin, donation drive, or home with a signed note), the user, which two of the four stitches and where, how much to add (a reinforcement, a second row of stitching, a detail, or a second small item), and how to present (a rotation, one partner, or one-on-one with me).

ELL and IEP supports: the picture plan template, where the brief and plan are a labeled drawing with number blanks and are scored on the same criterion 1 descriptors; sentence starters in Spanish, all to be verified by a native speaker; the reduced-scope version written on day 136 so no decision is made under pressure; the reduced-dexterity kit (self-threading needles, large-eye needles with heavier thread, threaders, pre-threaded needles at my table, binder clips instead of pins, a clamp or tape to hold the fabric, felt instead of fraying woven fabric, glue or hem tape on one of the two joins, a larger item with longer stitches), which never appears on a score; pre-measured and pre-marked material on request; the no-presentation option (the card plus a one-on-one 45 seconds, offered privately, scored identically); extended time into the next lesson's first minutes and the day 140 finishing window; and a written alternative for an absence on a build day. Full list in Project 05, Supports.

How it is presented: three tables, items and display cards only, no bodies and no modeling. Nobody wears anything in front of the room unless they ask to. Each maker gives the 45 seconds three or four times to whoever is standing at their item. Three named vote categories keep neatness from winning everything: Most Useful, Most Surprising Material, Best Construction. Every item is photographed with its display card, no faces and no names, and that photo set is the Unit 5 page on the website, the hallway display case, and next year's Lesson 5.15 slides at once.

Full no-machine version: the project runs entirely by hand, exactly as it did before the D19 revision, because a machine is an option here, never a requirement. What does need a stated alternative is dexterity, not machinery, and the reduced-dexterity kit above is required equipment on both build days, not an accommodation to be found on the day.

11. Health and other-department coordination

Art. Two real overlaps. Color, texture, and pattern are the same vocabulary the art teacher uses and the same vocabulary Unit 4 already taught in the Color in the Home work; in this unit they land on fabric instead of walls, in Lesson 5.3's swatch handling and Lesson 5.16's design decisions, and Lesson 5.16 cites National FCS 16.3.3 on the elements and principles of design in constructing and altering apparel. The running stitch is a drawn line, and the art teacher's line-quality words (even, steady, weight) transfer directly to Lesson 5.6's three criteria. FACS gives art a set of practice cloths and a gallery of twenty-one made objects; art gives FACS its own words for color, texture, and pattern so the two rooms say the same thing, and possibly a hand on the day 140 display setup.

Science. Two places. Fibers are materials science: Lesson 5.3's six-swatch test is a properties investigation, polyester and nylon are polymers, and the burn demonstration belongs in a fume hood more than in a FACS room. Lesson 5.4's stain station is applied chemistry: a base for grease, an acid for the juice, a solvent for the ink, three treatments on one stain with everything else held the same. FACS gives science a ready properties lab, a set of labeled swatches, and a class that has already run a three-treatment comparison; science gives FACS the fume hood, its own words for variable and control, and the microfiber content that Lesson 5.14 names.

Math. Lesson 5.13 is division with a unit written on the answer, Lesson 5.12 is a total with shipping and tax, Lesson 5.9 is a break-even comparison, and Lessons 5.10 and 5.16 are measurement to the quarter inch, seam allowance, finished size against cut size, and perimeter. Use the math class's own setup and words: cost per wear as a unit rate, stitches per inch as a rate, the plan sheet as a scale drawing at one square to one inch. FACS gives math four real costing problems with garments on the table and a scale drawing that gets built; math gives FACS students who already divide correctly and, if willing, ten minutes on day 133 to check the cost-per-wear work.

The school nurse. Notify the nurse before day 121, the first day needles come out, and tell her three things: which twelve days of the calendar have needles and pins in the room, what the count-out and count-in routine is and that no student is dismissed on a count that does not match, and that there is a hard plastic sharps jar in the room for bent needles and broken pins. Ask her two things: whether any student on the roster has a nickel or latex sensitivity, which decides the thimbles and the pins, and what she wants done for a needle stick, in her words, so the answer is the same in her office and in my room. FACS gives the nurse a written routine and twelve dated days; the nurse gives FACS a medical protocol and a roster check. This is not a courtesy, it is the one conversation in this unit that has to happen before the unit starts.

The custodian. Two asks, both small and both early. The scrap bin and the donation drive bin sit in the room from day 116 to day 141 and they need to not be emptied as trash, so tell him what they are and put a sign on both. And the floor: sweep the room before day 121 so a needle found later belongs to this unit, and expect thread on the floor for ten teaching days. FACS gives the custodian a room that gets cleaned to a routine by students every sewing day and a class that picks up what it drops; the custodian gives FACS the two bins, a warning before any floor work, and (in Lesson 5.1) the loan of a high-visibility vest and a work glove for the PPE table.

Health. The overlap is narrow and it is worth naming: the class agreement in Lesson 5.2 is about what students say to each other about appearance, which touches body image and belonging. Anything a student says about being commented on at home or online is a quiet referral to the counselor, not a class discussion, and the lesson says so. The counselor may want a copy of the agreement; give them one. This unit does not teach body image, dietary guidance, or self-esteem content; the health course owns those.

Physical education. The PE teacher already enforces footwear and can co-sign the closed-toe rule in one sentence at the door on day 116, which makes the FACS rule read as a building rule instead of a FACS rule.

Technology or makerspace, if the building has one. Their tool rules and their tool count are the same idea as ours. Use the same words in both rooms so a student hears one standard, and ask whether their teacher will open a sewing machine cover for ten minutes on Path A, because a machine is a mechanism with a cam, a linkage, and a feed.

The job

Find a used textile that was headed for the trash. Turn it into something a real person will actually use. Build it by hand in two class periods, using at least two of the four stitches you already know. Then explain it in 45 seconds to people who were not there while you made it.

You are not making a craft to take home and put in a drawer. You are making something with a user. The rubric asks who that person is, and "me" counts only if you can say what you will use it for.

The four constraints

Every project, every path, no exceptions.

  1. It must start from a used textile. Something somebody already owned: a t-shirt, jeans, a towel, a pillowcase, a sweater, a sheet, fabric scraps. Not new fabric off a roll.
  2. It must be finished in two build days. Lesson 5.17, two periods of 40 minutes. Your plan has to fit that, and your reduced-scope version is your insurance.
  3. It must use at least two of the taught joins: running stitch, backstitch, whipstitch, sewing on a button, or a machine seam reverse-locked at both ends. Fabric glue and hem tape can hold one join if you need them, but two joins have to be stitched, by hand or on the machine.
  4. It must be something somebody would actually use, and you have to name who. A name, and what they will use it for.

No sewing machine is needed anywhere in this project; every path can be built by hand. Since the machine block in Topic 5.2, the machines are also open on both build days under the same rules, and a machine seam counts as a taught join. Your plan sheet says which joins you are using, hand or machine, before anything is cut.

Teacher notes on the project

  • This is the unit's synthesis project and it is deliberately smaller in scope than the Unit 4 room design. Unit 4 asked students to plan a space on paper; this one asks them to make a physical object that works, with their hands, in 80 minutes of build time. The whole design is built around that constraint: an approval gate before anyone cuts, a milestone at the end of each build day, a stuck protocol so one teacher can serve 21 makers, and a reduced-scope version written on day one so no student has to invent a fallback while panicking.
  • The state's Sustainability theme module names this project almost exactly. Its Family and Consumer Sciences illustrative activity ends in "a recycle/upcycle/repurposing project, such as recycling t-shirts into reusable shopping bags," which is path 1 on the menu. The ATPD content module line 2. d), "Develop sustainability practices for repurposing apparel items," is the only place in the whole framework where the words repurposing apparel appear, and this project is the answer to it.
  • The scrap bin is the single most important piece of preparation. Start it on day 116, the first day of the unit, with a sign. By day 136 it should hold more usable pieces than there are students. A bin with four pieces in it makes twenty students fight, and it makes the students with nothing at home feel it.
  • Say the default out loud on day 136, before anyone asks: "Most people will use the bin. That is the normal way to do this project." That one sentence is why the no-item-from-home path works.
  • The approval gate is not optional. Fabric shears live at the teacher's table until a plan is initialed. Five questions, sixty seconds, every student: used material, two taught stitches, cut size includes the seam allowance, order of work is possible, reduced scope written. That conversation prevents almost every failure this project can have.
  • Scope is the enemy. The students who finish with nothing are the ones who planned a backpack, a quilt, or a stuffed animal. Constraint 2 and the reduced-scope line exist for them. Steer on day 136, not on day 139.
  • Individual work with station partners is intentional. A group project on a sewing build produces one student sewing and three watching. Everyone makes their own item; the station is for help, tools, and peer review.
  • Criterion 4 is scored from the clipboard across four days, not from one observation. Three needle and pin counts per day (one per station) are the evidence, and a student who runs a count correctly as Station Manager gets that noted. Rotate Station Manager daily so three different students run a count each day.
  • Grade the item on what it is, not on how ambitious it was. A whipstitched phone sleeve with even stitches and a working fit scores higher than a half-built pencil case with a great idea. Say that on day 136 and students choose honestly.
  • Photograph every item with its display card on day 141, no names and no faces. That photo set is the Unit 5 page on the website, the hallway display case, and next year's idea menu slides.
  • Material cost for the unit budget. The textiles are free (scrap bin plus donation drive). The consumables this project adds beyond the Topic 5.2 sewing kit: cotton cord or shoelaces for drawstring bags (a spool, about $4), assorted buttons (a jar, about $6, or free from the drive), uncooked rice (a two-pound bag, about $2), fabric glue (one bottle, about $4), iron-on hem tape (one roll, about $4), binder clips (a box of 12, about $2), card stock for display cards (one pack, about $6, or the copier's heaviest paper for free), butcher paper or three cloths for the display tables (about $8 or free). Total new spend for the project, about $20 to $36 for a class of 21, or under $2 per student. Everything else (needles, pins, thread, shears, seam rippers, thimbles, threaders, chalk) is already in the Topic 5.2 line and is reused every year.

Translation status. The Spanish column is my best working translation and has not been checked by a native speaker. Tool and stitch names are the worst case. Sewing words drift by country and by trade: what one country calls a stitch, another calls something else, and a few of these have no single everyday word in Spanish. The English term stays on every card, because a student will see the English word on a real care label, a real pattern envelope, a real tool, and a real store tag.

Two words do double duty and the table says which meaning is meant. "Pattern" here is the paper shape you cut fabric from, not a repeating print (the repeating print is the art word, and it belongs to Unit 4).
