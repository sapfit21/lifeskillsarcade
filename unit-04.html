1. Why this unit

I have been inside more buildings than most teachers, and not as a guest. For several summers I managed a hotel property. That job taught me that a building is not a thing. It is a system of small repairs and energy bills. A running toilet on the third floor is a water bill. A door that does not seal is a heating bill. A breaker that trips every Saturday night is a call I have to make. Nobody teaches that in school, and then people grow up and rent an apartment and do not know which way to turn the shutoff valve.

In college I worked on a zero-energy home project, and that is where I first saw that the design of a house and the cost of running it are the same problem. In my certification program my graded lesson was Color in the Home, where students painted a paper wall with frosting they mixed themselves and then pitched the room to the class. In my classroom I wrote ten interior design packets, from the eight elements to a scale floor plan of your own bedroom to a room built from secondhand and DIY only. This unit takes all of that and puts it in one place for 11 to 14 year olds.

Here is what I believe. A kid who can read a floor plan, fix a running toilet, and cut an electric bill has power at home. Not power over anyone. Power to help. Most of my students live in apartments or shared houses where a parent works two jobs and a repair waits until somebody can pay for it. If my student can find the breaker, plunge the sink, set the thermostat, and tell the landlord in one clear sentence what is wrong, that family is better off by Friday.

The unit runs in three moves. First the management half: what a home has to do for the people in it, what can hurt you at home, how to do the seven small repairs that come up most, and how to cut the energy and water a home uses. Then the design half: the elements and principles, color mixed by hand with frosting, texture and pattern, and a floor plan drawn to scale. Then the project: a real client with a real need, a scale plan with every path measured, a furniture budget with tax, a model built from a shoebox, and a 60-second pitch to a classmate who plays the client. Every day from the floor plan lesson to the last build day opens with a floor plan sketch, and that stack of sketches is turned in with the project. By the end, a student has drawn eleven rooms, done five repairs, audited a home, and defended a design to a room full of people.

2.3 Trends, issues, and news

  1. Energy prices and heat pumps. Students hear parents talk about the electric bill and the oil bill. New York State is pushing heat pumps and paying part of the cost through NYSERDA programs, and some students' homes have already switched. Lesson 4.4 puts a heat pump next to a furnace and a window unit and asks what each one costs to run.
  2. Tiny homes and accessory dwelling units. Students have seen tiny house videos. Long Island towns have been arguing about whether a family can put an apartment over the garage or in the basement. Lesson 4.1 uses both as housing types, and the Tiny House path in the project is the state's own illustrative activity turned into a design problem with a bigger budget and a harder footprint.
  3. Multigenerational households on Long Island. A grandparent moving in, or two families sharing a house because of rent, is normal in many schools. Lesson 4.1 counts it as a housing need across the lifespan (the no-perfect-fit family on the listings sheet is three generations on one income), and Lesson 4.11's Okafor card is a grandfather with a walker moving into a bedroom that was not built for him.
  4. Universal design and aging in place. Lever handles, no-step entries, 36-inch paths, and a shower with no lip help a grandparent, a person in a wheelchair, and a parent carrying groceries. Lesson 4.1 names universal design; Lesson 4.12 makes every path on the plan pass a 30-inch or 36-inch rule and draws the 60-inch turning circle; the last do-now sketch in 4.14 puts a turning circle in any room.
  5. Smart home devices. Thermostats you set from a phone, plugs that turn off on a schedule, doorbell cameras. Lesson 4.4 asks whether a smart thermostat saves money or just looks like it does; Lesson 4.16 asks what a house full of these devices means for the electrician and the energy auditor.
  6. Secondhand furniture apps. Students already know the apps where a desk sells for $40 and a couch goes free if you can carry it. Lesson 4.13's catalog prices every item three ways (new, secondhand, DIY) at realistic 2026 Long Island figures, and the hook is one desk at $149, $40, and $35 in parts.

2.4 What students this age find interesting

  1. Their own bedroom, and the fact that somebody is finally asking what they would change about it.
  2. Fixing something that adults usually pay someone to fix, and being able to say "I know how to do that." The $185 plumber's invoice for an $8 part is the hook of Lesson 4.3 for that reason.
  3. Frosting. Mixing it, painting with it, and defending the color to the class.
  4. Building a model with their hands and walking around a room full of them.
  5. The electric bill. Every student has heard someone at home complain about it; this is the unit that says what a kilowatt-hour is and which appliance is the problem.
  6. A client with a problem to solve, a budget that is real, and a classmate who gets to ask "where do I put my walker at night?"

3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

Big ideas:

  • A home has jobs to do, and the jobs change as the people in it change. A good home is not the biggest or the newest one. It is the one that does its jobs for the people who live there.
  • A home is a system. Every part of it costs something to run and something to fix, and small habits change the bill.
  • Most home injuries are preventable, and the person who prevents them is usually the one who notices first.
  • Good design is not decoration. It is a plan that fits the people who use the space, the way they move through it, and the money they have.
  • Color, light, and arrangement change how a person feels in a room, and a designer chooses them on purpose.
  • A designer does not design for themselves. Every space has a client, and the client's needs come before the designer's taste.

Essential questions:

  1. What does a home have to do for the people who live in it, and who decides when it is doing enough?
  2. What in a home can I fix myself, and how do I know when to call someone?
  3. Where does the energy and water in my home go, and what would cut the bill?
  4. Why does one room feel calm and another feel loud, before anyone says a word?
  5. How do you design a room for somebody who is not you?
  6. What does it cost to furnish a room, and what do I give up to stay under budget?

4. Knowledge and skills

Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):

  • The seven jobs of a home (shelter, safety, storage, rest, work, play, belonging) and the state's bigger idea that school, a job, and the neighborhood are part of a person's living space.
  • Housing needs across the lifespan: a baby, a teen, a grandparent, a wheelchair user; what each one adds to a plain two-bedroom apartment; universal design as designing once so nobody has to change it later.
  • Types of housing: apartment, single-family house, condo, townhouse, manufactured or mobile home, shared housing; rent versus own; what a lease, a landlord, and a mortgage are; why neither is "better."
  • The common hazards by room: kitchen (burns, grease, knives in the sink), bathroom (wet floor, no grab bar, medicine in reach, a hair dryer near water), stairs (clutter, a loose rug, no rail, dark), bedroom (a space heater by the bed, cords under the rug, a candle, a blocked window), garage (gasoline near the water heater, a running car), outside (a ladder left up, a pool with no gate, ice); the seven hazard types (burn, fall, fire, electrical, poison, drowning, other).
  • What a smoke detector and a carbon monoxide detector do, where they go (every level and every sleeping area), and when to test them (monthly), replace batteries (yearly), and replace the unit (ten years); the Poison Control number.
  • The three parts of a family emergency plan: two ways out of every bedroom, one meeting spot outside that everyone can reach (never the driveway), and the three numbers to call.
  • The seven small repair jobs: change a light bulb (switch off, cool, check the max watts, LED), reset a tripped breaker (find the one in the middle, fully off, then on, and stop if it trips again), unclog a drain without chemicals, stop a running toilet (the chain, the flapper, the float), tighten a loose screw (match the driver to the head), hang something on a wall (stud or anchor, level, slight upward angle), clean a filter (furnace, range hood, dryer lint).
  • The tools: Phillips and flathead screwdrivers, pliers, an adjustable wrench, a level, a tape measure, a sink plunger and a toilet plunger (the flange is the difference); the safety rules at a station.
  • The six-step troubleshooting process the state names: what is the problem, what should it do, test to find the cause, fix it, check that the fix worked, stop it happening again.
  • The maintenance calendar: monthly, seasonal, and yearly tasks and why each one lands where it does.
  • Energy at home: the sources (electricity, natural gas, oil, propane, sun), renewable or not; what a watt and a kilowatt-hour are; phantom load; LED versus older bulbs; thermostat settings; insulation and air leaks; heat pumps; what one appliance costs to run for a month.
  • Water at home: gallons per minute at a faucet and a shower; the running toilet as the largest silent leak; what a low-flow fixture does.
  • Waste, reuse, and materials: reduce, reuse, recycle, compost; what a landfill costs the community; a carbon footprint in 20 words; green materials and secondhand furnishings.
  • The eight elements of design: line, shape, form, space, texture, pattern, color, light.
  • The principles of design: balance (symmetrical and asymmetrical), focal point (emphasis), rhythm, proportion and scale, harmony (unity).
  • Color: the wheel, primary, secondary, tertiary, tint, shade, warm, cool, a palette, a scheme (monochromatic, complementary, analogous), the mood a color sets in a room.
  • Texture and pattern: how to layer them without chaos; what a mood board is for.
  • The floor plan: scale (1/4 inch = 1 foot to learn on; 1/2 inch = 1 foot for the project), the symbols for walls, doors with the swing arc, windows, and closets, zones (sleep, work, store, move), traffic flow, and clearance.
  • The traffic flow rules: 36 inches for any path a walker or wheelchair uses and for the front entry, 30 inches everywhere else, a 60-inch turning circle for a wheelchair, the door swing clear, nothing in front of a window that must open, one open side of a bed.
  • The design process the state names: define the problem, set criteria and constraints, brainstorm, select, prototype, test, optimize, evaluate; a client, a brief, a need (the design fails without it) versus a want (the design is better with it), and hidden needs.
  • A furnishings budget: the client's cap ($400 for a room refresh, $1,500 for the tiny house interior), a catalog with three prices per item (new, secondhand, DIY), sales tax at the county rate, a running total, and the trade-off box.
  • The project chain: architect, interior designer, contractor, electrician, plumber, HVAC technician, home inspector, energy auditor, sustainability consultant, landscape designer, furniture maker, real estate agent, property manager; the three paths into them; what each pays, to be refreshed before teaching.

Skills (what students will be able to do):

  • List the seven jobs of a home with an example of each; name three needs a baby, a teen, a grandparent, or a wheelchair user adds to a plain apartment.
  • Match six family cards to six home listings with a sentence per match that names a need, not a preference, and name what is still missing for the family with no perfect fit.
  • Read a described home and find fifteen hazards, classify each by type, and write the fix; say which is most dangerous for a toddler and which for a grandmother with a cane.
  • Draw a family emergency plan on the described floor plan with two exits per bedroom, a meeting spot that works for a cane, and the three numbers.
  • Name seven tools from the tray and match each to a job; at five stations, drive and remove a screw in a pilot hole with safety glasses on, seat a plunger and feel the seal, measure five lines to the nearest eighth of an inch, level a bracket and hang a frame, find the tripped breaker in the photo and say the reset in order, and change a bulb after reading the max watts; pass the test on every station card.
  • Say the six troubleshooting steps in order and run a running toilet through them.
  • Place fifteen maintenance tasks on a twelve-month grid and justify three placements by cause and season.
  • Run a home energy audit on a checklist: count bulbs by type, find phantom loads, read the thermostat, check the windows and doors for leaks, time the shower; read a plug-in watt meter on three devices; compute what one change saves in a month.
  • Sort 20 household items into reduce, reuse, recycle, compost, or trash and defend the calls; define a carbon footprint in 20 words; name a green material and a secondhand source for four furnishings.
  • Find each of the eight elements in a photo of a real room and in the FACS room itself.
  • Classify eight room layouts as symmetrical or asymmetrical, name the focal point in each, and explain the rhythm and proportion in two of them.
  • Mix a secondary color, a tint, and a shade from primaries in frosting; plan a palette for an assigned Room Card and mood; paint a wall panel; pitch it in 30 seconds with a family or function reason.
  • Build a mood board (color, texture, pattern, one furnishing) for a room and a mood.
  • Read a floor plan (symbols, scale, dimensions), find the traffic path, and draw a bedroom at 1/4 inch = 1 foot with the door swing right.
  • Sketch a floor plan from a prompt in a timed do now, eleven times, from the FACS room to a folded-down net of the client's room to an elevation at 1/2 inch = 1 foot to a room with a wheelchair turning circle in it.
  • Read a client card and identify the client, the room dimensions, the budget, and the one rule that cannot be broken; sort every line into a need or a want; add two hidden needs; write the design problem as "___ needs a room that ___ within ___" and defend it.
  • Draw the client's room outline at 1/2 inch = 1 foot with the door swing, the windows, the closet, the scale, and the client name; place 30 cut-out pieces in zones; shade and measure every path in inches and circle the ones that fail; draw the turning circle if the client uses a wheelchair; run a peer review checklist on another client's plan (mark, do not fix); revise and write two changes made and one suggestion rejected.
  • Price every piece on the plan from the catalog in a new, secondhand, or DIY column, add sales tax, keep a running total at or under the client's cap, and justify three purchase decisions with the five-step model.
  • Build a shoebox or foam board model at a stated scale that matches the plan, furnish it from scrap, apply the color scheme, add one texture, and label it GREEN and SAFE with a reason for this client and a justification card for the biggest design choice.
  • Deliver the five-part pitch in 60 seconds (the client and the need; the plan and the path; the budget total and the hardest trade-off; the color scheme and the mood; the green feature and the safe feature), answer the client's one question in one sentence, walk the gallery and write four two-stars-and-a-step feedback slips, and self-score on Rubric 04 with one line of evidence per criterion.
  • Sort thirteen career cards by path and by workplace, compare three careers on the Project Chain sheet, and rate their own fit for two of them with evidence from the project.

5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

  • Topic 4.1: Students will analyze a home for its seven jobs, its hazards, its energy and water use, and its waste using a family-to-home match, a paper safety hunt with an emergency plan, five repair stations, a maintenance calendar, and a home energy audit, and evaluate which three changes would make that home safer and cheaper to run, with numbers.
  • Topic 4.2: Students will evaluate real rooms for the elements and principles of design, create a color palette by mixing frosting to match a room and a mood and justify it in a pitch, and draw a bedroom floor plan to scale with correct symbols and a working traffic path.
  • Topic 4.3: Students will design a room for a client by writing the design problem, drawing a scale plan with every path measured against the traffic flow rules, constructing a furnishings budget with tax under the client's cap, building a model that matches the plan, and justifying the design to the client's needs in a five-part 60-second pitch, then evaluate four classmates' designs on feedback slips and their own on the rubric.
  • Topic 4.4: Students will compare thirteen careers in the housing project chain by role, path, and pay, and assess their own suitability for two of them with evidence from what they did in the project.

8. Project

Name: Design a Room. Full sheet in Project 04 - Design a Room.md (handed out with Rubric 04 in Lesson 4.11 so nothing on the rubric is a surprise, reread in 4.14 Day 3 and 4.15); rubric in Rubric 04 - Design a Room.md.

The scenario: Every student is a designer with one client, and the client is not them. In Lesson 4.11 each table reads six client cards aloud; each card has a person, a room with its dimensions, a budget, must-haves, nice-to-haves, and one rule that cannot be broken. The landed lesson names the Okafor card (a grandfather who uses a walker, likes blue, needs a 36-inch path from the door to the bed and a chair with arms) and the Rivera card (a wheelchair user; every path is 36 inches and that number is not up for discussion), a shared sibling room (Luz, eight, is the sister who is not the main client), a baby's room with a crib, a tiny house couple, and an entry with a coat hook that has to work for everyone. Two paths: Design Your Own Bedroom for a client (a room refresh, $400 cap: the walls and floor are there, the money buys furniture, storage, light, paint, and finish) or Tiny House (the state's own illustrative activity; the harder path, $1,500 for the interior, with a kitchen and a bathroom to fit and a loft drawn as a second plan). A student may switch cards up to the end of Lesson 4.12 Day 1 with no penalty.

Deliverables (the turn-in stack, in the order Lesson 4.15 sets): (1) the client card; (2) the Needs and Wants List with every card line sorted, two hidden needs, and the design problem sentence "___ needs a room that ___ within ___"; (3) the scale plan at 1/2 inch = 1 foot (tiny house teams may use 1/4 inch on letter paper) with the door swing, windows, closet, scale, and client name, the 30 cut-out pieces glued down in zones, every path shaded and measured in inches, the turning circle if the client uses a wheelchair, the peer review checklist, and on the back two changes made and one suggestion rejected, with reasons; (4) the Budget Sheet: every piece on the plan priced from the 40-item catalog in a new, secondhand, or DIY column, sales tax at the county rate, a running total at or under the cap, the trade-off box, and three purchase justifications; (5) Rubric 04 with the self-assessment row filled, one score and one line of evidence per criterion; (6) the four feedback slips collected from the cup at the team's station; (7) each student's floor-plan do-now stack, stapled in order with the name on the first sheet. The model stays on the shelf: a shoebox or foam board box at a stated scale that matches the plan, furniture built from scrap to scale, the color scheme from Lesson 4.8's color card applied, one texture, a GREEN label and a SAFE label each with a reason for this client, and the justification card for the biggest design choice. The pitch is live: five parts in 60 seconds to a classmate holding the client card, then one client question answered in one sentence.

Steps by day: Day 1 (Lesson 4.11, day 106): the Okafor card read as a designer reads it; the six cards read aloud; the client, the path, and alone or a team of two chosen; the Needs and Wants List; the design problem sentence, swapped and questioned. Days 2 and 3 (4.12, days 107 and 108): the tape strips on the floor; the outline at project scale from the card's numbers; zones and cut-outs, needs first, traced or taped; the traffic flow rules; every path shaded and measured; the peer review checklist; revise, glue, and write the two changes and the rejection. Day 4 (4.13, day 109): the catalog, the three columns, the tax, the running total, the trade-off box, three justifications with the five-step model. Days 5 to 7 (4.14, days 110 to 112): the box measured and marked to the plan and the walls up; furniture from scrap in ninety seconds each, the floor covering, the color scheme and one texture; the GREEN and SAFE labels, the justification card, the self-score on criterion 4. Day 8 (4.15, day 113): the pitch planner and one rehearsal; two rounds of pitches at the stations with assigned clients; the gallery walk with four slips; the self-assessment row; the turn-in stack. Anything unfinished on the labels finishes in the first ten minutes of 4.15; late projects take the one-level drop the grading plan sets, never a zero.

Group or individual: alone or a team of two, chosen by the student with my veto (or assigned by client card in a class that cannot handle choosing). A team turns in one plan, one Budget Sheet, one model, one rubric, and two do-now stacks; every teammate says at least one part of the pitch. A student who cannot build submits the isometric grid drawing from the build guide in place of the model, scored on the same criterion.

Choice built in: the client card and the path; alone or a team; new, secondhand, or DIY for every piece; the model medium (a shoebox, foam board, or the isometric drawing); a larger scale (1 inch = 1 foot on 11 by 17) for a student who needs it; the pitch read from the planner, given to the teacher alone at the station, or given with a teammate reading while the student points; the color scheme from the student's own Lesson 4.8 card or one of the three on the build guide.

ELL and IEP supports: the client cards carry a picture line at the top (a walker, a desk, a coat hook, a crib, a tiny house, a wheelchair) so the client can be identified before the text is read; the Needs and Wants List, the plan guide, and the pitch planner carry sentence frames and the key words in Spanish (Vocabulary 04.md), to be verified by a native speaker; the problem sentence may be written in the home language first; the room outline can be pre-drawn and the furniture pre-cut; a printed tax table on the back of the Budget Sheet and a calculator; pre-cut wall pieces and pre-folded bed and desk blanks; a student who cannot use scissors or glue safely directs a partner's hands and owns the labels and the justification; two feedback slips instead of four; extended time into the next lesson at every step (the list into 4.12, the revision into 4.13, the budget into 4.14, the labels into 4.15, the self-assessment into 4.16).

How it is presented: Lesson 4.15. Stations are set so no two teams with the same client card sit together, and each team is assigned a "client," a student from another team who holds the card and listens as that person. Half the teams pitch while the other half are clients; at sixty seconds the timer sounds and the pitch stops; the client asks one question from the card ("Where do I put my walker at night?" "Can I reach the closet rod?"); the team answers in one sentence; then the halves swap. Then everyone walks: at four models that are not their own client, read the card, look at the plan and the model, and fill a slip with two stars (two things this design does for its client, specific) and one step. "I like it" does not count. A local interior designer, architect, or contractor as a guest client for the period, asking the questions, is the best version of this lesson (Section 12). The grade is the rubric: criterion 1 the client fit, from the Needs and Wants List and the pitch; 2 the plan; 3 the Budget Sheet; 4 the model and the design principles, from the model and the Day 3 clipboard check; 5 the pitch, from the clipboard checklist and the self-assessment, where a self-score within one level of mine on every line is one of the Exceeds conditions. A plain model with a plan that fits the client and a budget that adds up scores higher than a beautiful model that ignored the brief. I say that on day 1 of the project and again before the pitches.

Teacher notes on the project

  • This is the synthesis project from my Dream Home Challenge packet, rebuilt with a client, a budget, a model, and a pitch, and spread across a 40-minute daily schedule with a checkpoint every day. My packet asked students to design a room of their own; this version asks them to design for someone else, which is the difference between EDM 2. a) (shared space, privacy needs, group needs) and a decoration exercise. The state's own sample learning experiences for FACS include "Design Your Own Bedroom" (6 days) and "Tiny Houses" (9 days); both are offered as paths here in 8 days.
  • The six client cards deliberately include a walker user, a wheelchair user, and a new baby, because the state's Health, Safety, and Wellness illustrative activity for this module asks for exactly that: a floor plan revised for a family member who needs special consideration. The numbers on the cards (36-inch path, 60-inch turning circle, bed at chair height, rod at 48 inches) come from the 2010 ADA Standards and common universal design guidance.
  • Scale: Lesson 4.10 taught 1/4 inch = 1 foot. The project plan uses 1/2 inch = 1 foot so a bedroom fills a sheet of quarter-inch graph paper (two squares per foot). The Unit 4 Test asks both conversions. The model uses the plan's scale or 1 inch = 1 foot, written on the model.
  • Budget for the unit plan, per model: shoebox free (collected from students, families, and a shoe store from Lesson 4.6 onward); foam board about $3 to $4 per 20 by 30 sheet, two or three bases per sheet if boxes run short; card stock about $0.30; glue sticks about $0.25; scrap fabric and paint chips free from a fabric store's remnant bin and a paint department; masking tape and markers from room stock. Target under $4 per model, realistically $2 to $3.50. For 25 models, about $60 to $90 if every student needs a foam base, about $20 if the boxes come in.
  • The catalog prices in Handout 04.13 are realistic 2026 figures for Long Island, every one to be refreshed before teaching. The tax math works at any rate; only the teacher key's answers move.
  • A team of two is a choice, not a default. In a class that cannot choose well, assign partners by client card and say so on day 1.
  • Photograph every model, no names, no faces. They are the website's Unit 4 page and the principal's shelf.
