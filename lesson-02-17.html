PI D reads "Students understand how working contributes to a quality living environment," which does not fit a lesson on a market crash, so it is not cited. The decision-making language the lesson needs is in the Standard 3 Key Idea ("make effective decisions"), quoted above. Standard 1 PI D ("Students apply the decision making process to dilemmas related to personal health") fits the stress half of this lesson, but the state assigns only Standards 2 and 3 to the IGLR module, so it is not cited either.

Enduring understanding and essential question

Big idea: A boom is when prices rise so long that everyone forgets they can fall. A crash is when they fall fast and everyone feels it at once. The feeling is real both times; the loss is only real if you sell, or if you borrowed. The people who do worst in a crash are usually the ones who decided fastest, in 1928 or in 1929.
Essential question: When your money is climbing and everyone is buying, or falling and everyone is selling, what is the smart thing to do, and how would you know?

Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. (Low) Given the Round 6 and Round 7 dispatches, describe what a boom, a margin loan and a market crash are, and name two signs of stress they noticed in themselves or the room.
  2. (Mid) Given their own ledger at 1928 prices and again at October 1929 prices, apply the five-step decision model (name the choice, list options, weigh each against what matters, decide, look back) to the sell, hold, buy or borrow choice and record it on the decision grid before trading.
  3. (High) Given the class poll and the two crashes on the class's own board (1893 and 1929), evaluate their own Round 6 and Round 7 decisions in writing: a plan or a rush, a plan or a panic, and what number from the ledger says so.

Vocabulary

Tier 2 (general academic): crisis, panic, evidence, recover
Tier 3 (FACS): boom, margin loan, margin call, market crash, panic selling, sell, hold, buy, loss aversion (grade 8 stretch only). See the unit vocabulary list for the Spanish column.

Pre-assessment

The Lesson 2.16 exit cards and pie sketches tell you who is walking in with one big slice. Those students, and anyone who asks about the margin line before you explain it, are the ones to watch in the first three minutes of Day 1, not to rescue, but to make sure they are writing before they trade.

Do now and hook (Time: 3 min, Day 1)

The Round 6 prices are on the board when students walk in, under the year 1928. On the board: "Write the Round 6 prices in your ledger. Work out your total. It is probably the highest it has ever been. Write one word for how you feel and one thing you want to do right now. Do not trade yet."

Debrief: collect the feeling words out loud, fast, and write them on the board (rich, excited, nervous, greedy, fine). The hook is the board itself: "Every one of those words has cost somebody real money. Today you learn to feel it and still think."

Questions to ask

Monitor understanding:

  • Which line on your ledger did not fall between Round 6 and Round 7? Why that one?
  • You held. Your total is down $600. Did you lose $600?
  • You borrowed $200 in Round 6 and bought 2 RADIO at 100. RADIO is 30. What does the broker want, and what do you have?
  • You sold everything at Round 7 prices. What would you need to see to buy back in?

Deepen learning:

  • Two students both held RADIO at 100. One sold at 30, one held. Which one made the decision, and which one had the decision made for them by their stomach?
  • The Round 7 dispatch says the factories are still there. So what changed between Round 6 and Round 7?
  • In 1893 RAIL fell from 45 to 25 and was 60 by 1901. Who was hurt worst then: the person who never bought RAIL, the one who held, or the one who sold at 25? What does that tell you about 1929?
  • What does a stress sign feel like in your body, and how would you know it before you write a sell in the log?

Check for understanding

Day 1, Step 2: clipboard yes or no per student on "grid has a Round 6 decision written before the first trade" and "the reason names a number or a risk level, not a feeling alone." Day 2, Step 8: yes or no on "look back names a piece of evidence." A "got it" look back sounds like: "Round 6 was a rush: I bought 3 RADIO at 100 because everyone was. Round 7 was a plan: I held, because 3 shares at 30 is $90 and selling would lock in a $210 loss, and the bank still has my $250."

Closure (Time: 4 min each day)

Day 1: two students read the "how I feel now" sentence from Entry 6. Exit card: "One stress sign I noticed today, and one thing I did or could do before a money decision." Sort into "got it" (names a sign and a step) and "needs more" (the health teacher's stress lesson covers the rest; flag the cards for them).
Day 2: exit card, 3-2-1: 3 things that happened in October 1929 from the dispatch, 2 lines on the ledger that held or fell least, 1 sentence: plan or panic, and why. Three students read the "1" aloud, from different corners if possible. Sort; a student who could not name a line that held gets the two-crash organizer with the "what held" column filled by the teacher.

Differentiation and supports

  • ELL: both dispatches are read aloud twice, once fast and once slow; the decision grid has the five steps as icons (a question mark, a list, a scale, a check mark, a mirror) and a word bank for feelings in English and Spanish (scared, worried, calm, angry, excited); sentence starter "I felt ___ when the prices dropped" is on the journal template; the two-crash organizer is a table, not a paragraph; the margin rule is on the grid as three short lines with a worked example.
  • IEP and 504 (general): a student who becomes distressed by the drop can turn the ledger face down and do the grid first with the teacher's numbers; the four-corners poll can be done seated with a hand vote; the two-crash notes can be a printed slide with two blanks each; the margin math box can be done with the teacher; extended time by finishing the look back at the start of Lesson 2.18. Tell the counselor's office in advance that this week is a designed stress moment in a game, so no one is surprised by what a student reports.
  • Grade 6 support: the grid weighs two options on each day (sell or hold on Day 2; hold or buy with cash on Day 1), and the margin line stays closed for the student unless they ask for it; the two crashes are taught as one story (1929) with 1893 in one sentence.
  • Grade 8 stretch: introduce the term loss aversion (losing $100 feels worse than winning $100 feels good) and ask the student to find one line in their journal that shows it; the student computes the percent drop of one company from Round 6 to Round 7 from the ledger (a preview of Lesson 2.18), and works out the largest margin loan that would still have survived Round 7 on their own ledger.
  • UDL checkpoint used: multiple means of engagement (a real feeling, named and managed, in a safe setting) and multiple means of action and expression (the grid can be written, dictated, or done with the icon strip and a check mark).
  • No-kitchen or no-machine alternative: not needed; the whole lesson is paper, a board and a room to stand up in. If the class cannot move for four corners, the poll is four hand votes.

Homework

None. Optional: ask an adult if they remember a time the stock market fell, or a family story about hard times handed down, and what people did with their money, if they are willing to say. No dollar amounts.

Connections

Health: the stress signs and the "stop, breathe, name the choice" step are the health teacher's territory (HSW 6). Coordinate the week so both classes use the same words; FACS gives health a live stress moment students can name, health gives FACS the calming techniques. Social studies: the 1920s boom and the crash of October 1929 are US history; the social studies teacher gets a class that has held RADIO at 100 and watched it go to 30.

Teacher notes

  • If Day 1 runs short, cut the journal entry to the three base sentences and the total, and take the feelings lines into the Day 2 do now. Never cut the grid before trading; a trade with no written decision is the rush the lesson is trying to catch.
  • The teacher ledger: load it with RADIO before Day 1 (say, 20 shares bought at 6 in Round 5, worth $2,000 at 100) so you have a real booming ledger to model the grid on, and a real crashing one on Day 2. Students watch you not panic either day.
  • The margin line is the whole reason Day 1 exists. Do not talk anyone out of borrowing. A student who borrows the maximum, buys RADIO at 100 and is wiped out at 30 has the best possible Entry 7, and the rubric scores the reasoning, not the total. Say once, clearly, what a margin call is; then let them decide.
  • Common mistake: a student who was wiped out or sold everything on Day 2 wants to "start over." No resets during the crash. The report they write about that decision is worth more than a clean leaderboard. Round 2 (Lessons 2.20 to 2.22) is the fresh $1,000.
  • Watch for a student who is genuinely upset, not game upset. It happens about once a year. The ledger goes face down, the grid gets done with you, and the leaderboard is not mentioned to that student again this week.
  • Do not tell the class what Round 8 brings. Some of them will read the last line of the Round 7 dispatch ("the factories are still there") and buy at 30; that is the best possible Entry 7. Some will read it and still be wrong for three more years. That is Lesson 2.18.
