1. Why this unit (rationale, in Sal's voice)

I spent ten years around money before I taught it. Penn State gave me an entrepreneurship degree and an MBA. Oracle taught me to read numbers for a living. New York Life and my FINRA licenses taught me how ordinary families actually save, borrow, and invest, and how often nobody explained any of it to them until it was late. When I started teaching, I looked for a tool that would let a 12-year-old feel what a market does to a person, and there was not one. So I built ClassroomStreet.com. More than 150 students have traded on it, and math, ELA, and history teachers have used it too.

This unit starts with the student, not the dollar. Before anyone touches a budget, students name what they value, find out what they are good at, and set a goal with a date on it. Then the money comes in: a real pay stub, a personal budget they build from Long Island prices and revise at the end of the unit, seven ways to pay, what a minimum payment really costs, what compound growth really earns, and a candy market that shows them who gets paid on every trade. Then they invest for eight trading days on ClassroomStreet, survive a crash, see what they were really buying, and write a report about what they did and why. The unit closes with the 16 career clusters and a career research card, which is where I ran resume workshops for 87 or more students at Brentwood and where Unit 7 picks up.

I believe money skills at 12 change what a kid thinks is possible. A student who can read a pay stub, balance a budget, and sit still through a fake market crash is not the same student in June. This is the unit where I use everything I did before teaching, and it is the unit I would show a principal first.

2.3 Trends, issues, and news

  1. Buy now, pay later apps. Students see "four payments of $12.50" on the checkout page of every sneaker and game site. Lesson 2.11 gives them the tool for it: a balance, a rate, a minimum payment, and what the total comes to. I name the apps in the debrief as one more kind of credit.
  2. Phone payment apps. Most students already send and receive money on a phone, often on a parent's account. Lesson 2.10 puts app payment next to cash, check, debit, credit, prepaid cards, and bank transfer and asks where the money comes from and what happens if the phone is lost.
  3. Side hustles and creator income. Students watch people their age get paid for videos, reselling, and gaming. Lesson 2.8 lists selling things you make, tutoring, and dog walking as earned income now; the question I ask is what those people actually do all day and what most of them earn.
  4. AI and jobs. Students hear that AI will take jobs. On the cluster walk in Lesson 2.21 the question for each poster is which of its three jobs a computer could do and which need a person in the room.
  5. The cost of living on Long Island. Rent, a car, and groceries here cost more than the national average. Lesson 2.9's scenario cards use Long Island prices for 2026 so the budget is not a fantasy.
  6. Sports betting ads. Students see them during every game. Lesson 2.14 puts every venture on a risk line from a savings account to a startup stock; a bet belongs past the far end of that line, and Lesson 2.13 shows who designed the game and why the house always gets paid.

3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

Big ideas:

  • Knowing what you value and what you are good at is the first step in every good decision, including money decisions.
  • A goal without a plan and a date is a wish.
  • Every dollar does one of three jobs: spend, save, or grow. A budget is the plan for which job each dollar gets, and savings goes first.
  • Borrowed money costs money. Interest works for you when you save and against you when you owe.
  • Markets go up and down. The person who panics loses; the person with a plan waits.
  • Every career has a path, and the path starts with what interests you now.

Essential questions:

  1. Who am I, and what do I want?
  2. How do I turn a wish into a goal I can actually hit?
  3. Where does money come from, where does it go, and who decides?
  4. What does it cost to borrow, and what does it pay to wait?
  5. When prices crash, what should I do?
  6. What work could I do, and what would it take to get there?

4. Knowledge and skills

Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):

  • What a value is, what a trait is, what a strength is, and the difference between an interest and a skill.
  • The six interest types (realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, conventional) and what a two-letter code means.
  • The five parts of a SMART goal; what a stretch goal is and when to use one; short term versus long term.
  • The five-step decision model from Unit 0 (name the choice, list options, weigh each against what matters, decide, look back).
  • Time and energy as resources that run out, and what a week looks like on paper.
  • Needs, wants, and the gray zone, and how a value moves a thing from one column to the other.
  • Sources of income, earned and not earned: allowance, chores, babysitting, dog walking, tutoring, selling things you make, an hourly job, a salary, interest on savings, a gift.
  • The parts of a pay stub: gross pay, deductions (federal tax, state tax, Social Security, Medicare, health insurance), net pay.
  • The parts of a budget: net income, savings first, fixed expenses, variable expenses, a bottom line at zero or above; what "balanced" means.
  • Seven ways to pay: cash, check, debit, credit, prepaid card, phone or app payment, bank transfer; where the money comes from for each and what risk each one carries.
  • How to write a check and keep a register; how to balance an account against a statement.
  • Credit, debt, debit, loan, interest, principal, APR, minimum payment, and the credit score (300 to 850).
  • Simple interest (I = P x r x t) versus compound growth; the Rule of 72 as a grade 8 stretch.
  • Saving versus investing; risk and reward; the seven ClassroomStreet ventures and what each one really is (a big tech stock, a startup or IPO, corporate bonds, U.S. Treasury bonds, an IRA, a high-yield savings account, a REIT).
  • Why prices move (a dispatch, supply and demand, fear and greed); what diversification is; what a crash and panic selling are; how to compute a percent return.
  • What a fee and a commission are, and why the house always gets paid.
  • Employability skills (soft skills) and the evidence rule: a skill you can name is a skill you can show with an example.
  • The 16 national career clusters; the six paths after high school (a job with on-the-job training, an apprenticeship, a certificate program, a two-year degree, a four-year degree, the military).

Skills (what students will be able to do):

  • Sort 24 value cards into matters a lot, some, and less; rank the top three and explain them in writing.
  • Complete a strengths inventory with notice cards from tablemates, take the interest quiz, and read a two-letter code into two careers.
  • Design a one-page All About Me (values, strengths, interests and letters, two careers, a "known for" sentence), present it to a partner in two minutes, and give one specific piece of written feedback.
  • Write a SMART goal and a stretch goal for the same wish and tell them apart.
  • Apply the five-step decision model to a real choice and write a Goal Plan with steps and dates.
  • Map a week's time and energy and find where the goal's hours come from.
  • Sort 30 item cards into need, want, or gray zone; with $100 and a price list, decide what to buy, skip, and save.
  • Read a pay stub, compute net pay from gross pay, and check the percent taken out.
  • Build a monthly budget from a scenario card that pays savings first, covers every fixed need, and does not spend more than the income; check a partner's.
  • Describe seven ways to pay; write a check; keep a register of ten transactions to the cent; choose the safest way to pay in four situations.
  • State four differences between a debit card and a credit card; compute simple interest at three rates; compare the $25 minimum payment to $50 a month on a $500 balance and recommend a plan with numbers.
  • Compute compound growth on the same $100 and explain why it beats simple interest.
  • Trade in the Skittles market, track the fees, and say who got paid.
  • Log in to ClassroomStreet with a merchant number and PIN, sort the seven ventures by risk, make first trades with a written reason, and keep the Investor Journal every window.
  • Read a dispatch, predict the price move, and check the prediction.
  • Rebalance or hold when one slice of the portfolio is bigger than half, and say why.
  • Fill the sell, hold, buy decision grid before a crisis window and follow it.
  • Compute the percent return on the portfolio and on one venture with the formula written out.
  • Write the Investor Report from the journal and the ledger, revise the personal budget with a savings line at the top, and check a partner's report.
  • Rate six soft skills honestly with evidence and write a growth plan for one.
  • Pick a top three from the 16 clusters matched to the interest code, research one career on a card with a source, and name the path from grade 8 to the job.

5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

  • Topic 2.1: Students will evaluate their own values, traits, strengths, and interests using a value card sort, a strengths inventory, and an interest quiz, and create a one-page All About Me that presents the results to a partner with evidence.
  • Topic 2.2: Students will design a Goal Plan for a real personal goal by writing a SMART goal and a stretch goal, applying the five-step decision model to the choice behind it, and justifying where the time and energy will come from.
  • Topic 2.3: Students will analyze a pay stub, a scenario budget, seven ways to pay, two credit payment plans, and a savings table, and create a balanced monthly personal budget that they revise at the end of the unit.
  • Topic 2.4: Students will make and justify investing decisions on ClassroomStreet across an eight-day, 40-window run that includes a market crash, compute the percent return, and evaluate their own decisions in an Investor Report with evidence from the Investor Journal and the ledger.
  • Topic 2.5: Students will assess their own employability skills and interests against the 16 career clusters, select a top three, and create a career research card with the path from grade 8 to one career.

8. Project

Name: ClassroomStreet Investor Report and Personal Budget. Full sheet in Project 02 - ClassroomStreet Investor Report and Personal Budget.md (handed out in Lesson 2.14 and reread in 2.19); rubric in Rubric 02 - Investor Report and Personal Budget.md. The paper ledger version of the whole run is in Project 02 and Handout 02.19 - Paper Ledger.md.

The scenario: Every student is a new merchant in the Golden Land with AQ25,000 on ClassroomStreet.com and seven ventures to choose from (Sparkstone Mines, Cloudberry Expeditions, The Iron Vault, Crown Seal Notes, Willowbrook Grove, The Honeypot Coffer, Meadowgold Acres). Students log in with a merchant number and a PIN; no name touches the board. For eight trading days (40 windows) the student reads the dispatch, decides what to buy, sell, or hold, and writes one Investor Journal entry saying why. Window 25 is the crisis: the risky ventures fall hard. The market freezes at window 40. On the closing bell the leaderboard goes up by merchant number, then the reveal: every venture mapped to its real instrument, AQ to dollars, the dispatches to financial news, the crisis to 2008 or 2020. Then the student writes it up on the report template and revises the personal budget from Lesson 2.9 with a savings line at the top. [Sal: the lessons use AQ25,000 and seven ventures, from your Desktop files; your August portfolio says the live site has ten ventures. Confirm the list, and any extra three need a line on the reveal slide.]

Deliverables: (1) the Investor Journal, Entries 1 to 10, one per class day plus the three short Round 2 lines, stapled behind the report; (2) the Investor Report on Handout 02.19 - Investor Report Template.md, seven parts: starting and ending balance; percent return with the formula written out; three decisions with a ledger number each and what happened; what the crisis taught me; one thing I would do differently; the reveal table; the revised personal budget with a savings line; (3) the sell, hold, buy decision grid from 2.17 and the Return Math handout from 2.18, stapled behind; (4) a peer read with three boxes checked and two comments, one specific.

Steps by day: Day 1 (Lesson 2.14, day 53): venture risk cards, login cards, first buys across at least three ventures with AQ1,000 kept in cash, windows 1 to 6 over two days. Day 2 (2.14, day 54): read the portfolio screen, Entry 2. Days 3 and 4 (2.15, days 55 and 56): news cards and price changes, the three tests on a headline, windows 7 to 18. Day 5 (2.16, day 57): three portfolios and a shock, the pie sketch, rebalance or hold, windows 19 to 24. Days 6 and 7 (2.17, days 58 and 59): the crisis at window 25, the decision grid, signs of stress, windows 26 to 32. Day 8 (2.18, day 60): return math, the recovery, windows 33 to 40, freeze. Day 9 (2.19, day 61): closing bell, leaderboard, reveal, three ledger paths, Entry 9. Day 10 (2.19, day 62): write the report, revise the budget, peer read, submit; part 7 may finish at the start of Lesson 2.20. Round 2 (2.20 to 2.22, days 63 to 65): windows 41 to 47, 48 to 54, and 55 to 60, ten minutes per period, on the same board after the reveal, with three short Round 2 lines in the journal; the leaderboard freezes before the test; Round 2 standings go up in the first 10 minutes of Unit 3 Day 1. [Sal: the Project 02 sheet is the authority on the due date and the Round 2 rules if it says otherwise.]

Group or individual: individual portfolio and individual report. Each student has a trading partner who checks the return math and does the peer read. Partners are the same pairs as the Skittles trades where that worked.

Choice built in: which ventures to hold and how many; hold or rebalance at every window; the form of the report (written on the template, typed, dictated to a partner, or recorded as a voice note with the template numbers on paper); which three decisions to write about; whether the revised budget's savings line goes to a savings account first and a slow, spread-out investment second, or to a goal from Lesson 2.5.

ELL and IEP supports: sentence starters for the journal and the report in Turkish, Portuguese, and Spanish (Vocabulary 02.md and the template boxes); the reveal slides with one picture per venture; the return math as a fill-in with the subtraction line and the division line in separate boxes; calculators for all; the paper ledger for any student without a device; the printed ledger path from the export in place of a lost journal; three decisions can be two; extended time into the Round 2 window; a seat away from the leaderboard on the closing bell if rank is a stressor; the report may be written in the first language with the numbers in English, marked [check with a native speaker] for scoring.

How it is presented: the closing bell debrief has two rules said before anyone speaks: nobody is named unless they name themselves, and nobody says "loser" about anyone. Two or three volunteers from the top and the bottom say what they did in one sentence. Three anonymized ledger paths from the export (one safe, one risky, one that sold at the bottom) are the texts the class reads. The grade is the rubric, not the rank: a student who finished last with a diversified portfolio, a calm crisis grid, correct math, and an honest report scores higher than a student who got lucky. I say that on day 1 and again on the closing bell. [Sal: your Fortune Street rubric scored participation, analysis and decision making, emotional control, and reflection; Rubric 02 keeps those four and adds the revised budget as the fifth criterion, 20 points.]

The paper ledger version (no devices)

How a day runs.

  1. The teacher reads the day's dispatch aloud from the script (twice, once slow).
  2. The teacher posts the day's seven prices on the board from the price table.
  3. Students update the "price now" column and the "value" column on their ledger sheet (units held times price now), and total their fortune (cash plus all values).
  4. Trading window: students write each trade on the ledger (ticker, buy or sell, units, price, cash in or out), then update cash and units. Whole units only. Cash may not go below zero.
  5. The teacher collects the sheets at the bell every day, or students keep them in the folder if the class can be trusted with its own prices.

The crisis on paper. Day 6 prices fall hard for SPRK and CLDB and a little for MDOW; SEAL and HNYP hold; the dispatch says why. Day 7 falls further. Day 8 recovers. The four-corners poll, the decision grid, and Return Math all work from the sheet.
