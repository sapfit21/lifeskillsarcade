1. Why this unit

I spent ten years around money before I taught it. College gave me an entrepreneurship degree and an MBA. Business development at a software company taught me to read numbers for a living. Financial services and my FINRA licenses taught me how ordinary families actually save, borrow, and invest, and how often nobody explained any of it to them until it was late. When I started teaching, I looked for a way to let a 12-year-old feel what a market does to a person. What I use now is a game on paper: the Gilded Age Market, eight rounds from 1870 to 1932 and a closing bell in 1936, with real companies, two panics, a trust, a boom on borrowed money and the crash of October 1929. Students learn history and investing at the same time, and nobody needs a device.

This unit starts with the student, not the dollar. Before anyone touches a budget, students name what they value, find out what they are good at, and set a goal with a date on it. Then the money comes in: a real pay stub, a personal budget they build from Long Island prices and revise at the end of the unit, seven ways to pay, what a minimum payment really costs, what compound growth really earns, and a candy market that shows them who gets paid on every trade. Then they invest through eight rounds of the Gilded Age Market, from 1870 to 1932, survive the crash of 1929, find out what happened next to every company they held, and write a report about what they did and why. The unit closes with the 16 career clusters and a career research card, which is where I ran resume workshops for 87 or more students and where Unit 7 picks up.

I believe money skills at 12 change what a kid thinks is possible. A student who can read a pay stub, balance a budget, and sit still through a fake market crash is not the same student in June. This is the unit where I use everything I did before teaching, and it is the unit I would show a principal first.

2.3 Trends, issues, and news

  1. Buy now, pay later apps. Students see "four payments of $12.50" on the checkout page of every sneaker and game site. Lesson 2.11 gives them the tool for it: a balance, a rate, a minimum payment, and what the total comes to. I name the apps in the debrief as one more kind of credit.
  2. Phone payment apps. Most students already send and receive money on a phone, often on a parent's account. Lesson 2.10 puts app payment next to cash, check, debit, credit, prepaid cards, and bank transfer and asks where the money comes from and what happens if the phone is lost.
  3. Side hustles and creator income. Students watch people their age get paid for videos, reselling, and gaming. Lesson 2.8 lists selling things you make, tutoring, and dog walking as earned income now; the question I ask is what those people actually do all day and what most of them earn.
  4. AI and jobs. Students hear that AI will take jobs. On the cluster walk in Lesson 2.21 the question for each poster is which of its three jobs a computer could do and which need a person in the room.
  5. The cost of living on Long Island. Rent, a car, and groceries here cost more than the national average. Lesson 2.9's scenario cards use Long Island prices for 2026 so the budget is not a fantasy.
  6. Sports betting ads. Students see them during every game. Lesson 2.14 puts every company card on a risk line from the savings bank to RADIO; a bet belongs past the far end of that line, and Lesson 2.13 shows who designed the game and why the house always gets paid.

3. Enduring understandings and essential questions

Big ideas:

  • Knowing what you value and what you are good at is the first step in every good decision, including money decisions.
  • A goal without a plan and a date is a wish.
  • Every dollar does one of three jobs: spend, save, or grow. A budget is the plan for which job each dollar gets, and savings goes first.
  • Borrowed money costs money. Interest works for you when you save and against you when you owe.
  • Markets go up and down. The person who panics loses; the person with a plan waits.
  • Every career has a path, and the path starts with what interests you now.

Essential questions:

  1. Who am I, and what do I want?
  2. How do I turn a wish into a goal I can actually hit?
  3. Where does money come from, where does it go, and who decides?
  4. What does it cost to borrow, and what does it pay to wait?
  5. When prices crash, what should I do?
  6. What work could I do, and what would it take to get there?

4. Knowledge and skills

Knowledge (facts, vocabulary, ideas):

  • What a value is, what a trait is, what a strength is, and the difference between an interest and a skill.
  • The six interest types (realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, conventional) and what a two-letter code means.
  • The five parts of a SMART goal; what a stretch goal is and when to use one; short term versus long term.
  • The five-step decision model from Unit 0 (name the choice, list options, weigh each against what matters, decide, look back).
  • Time and energy as resources that run out, and what a week looks like on paper.
  • Needs, wants, and the gray zone, and how a value moves a thing from one column to the other.
  • Sources of income, earned and not earned: allowance, chores, babysitting, dog walking, tutoring, selling things you make, an hourly job, a salary, interest on savings, a gift.
  • The parts of a pay stub: gross pay, deductions (federal tax, state tax, Social Security, Medicare, health insurance), net pay.
  • The parts of a budget: net income, savings first, fixed expenses, variable expenses, a bottom line at zero or above; what "balanced" means.
  • Seven ways to pay: cash, check, debit, credit, prepaid card, phone or app payment, bank transfer; where the money comes from for each and what risk each one carries.
  • How to write a check and keep a register; how to balance an account against a statement.
  • Credit, debt, debit, loan, interest, principal, APR, minimum payment, and the credit score (300 to 850).
  • Simple interest (I = P x r x t) versus compound growth; the Rule of 72 as a grade 8 stretch.
  • Saving versus investing; risk and reward; the eight holdings on the Gilded Age Market (BANK, RAIL, OIL, GAS, ELEC, STEEL, AUTO, RADIO), the real company behind each ticker, and what happened next to each one.
  • Why prices move (a dispatch, supply and demand, fear and greed); what a trust, a monopoly, and a margin loan are; what diversification is; what a panic, a crash, and panic selling are; how to compute a percent return.
  • What a fee and a commission are, and why the house always gets paid.
  • Employability skills (soft skills) and the evidence rule: a skill you can name is a skill you can show with an example.
  • The 16 national career clusters; the six paths after high school (a job with on-the-job training, an apprenticeship, a certificate program, a two-year degree, a four-year degree, the military).

Skills (what students will be able to do):

  • Sort 24 value cards into matters a lot, some, and less; rank the top three and explain them in writing.
  • Complete a strengths inventory with notice cards from tablemates, take the interest quiz, and read a two-letter code into two careers.
  • Design a one-page All About Me (values, strengths, interests and letters, two careers, a "known for" sentence), present it to a partner in two minutes, and give one specific piece of written feedback.
  • Write a SMART goal and a stretch goal for the same wish and tell them apart.
  • Apply the five-step decision model to a real choice and write a Goal Plan with steps and dates.
  • Map a week's time and energy and find where the goal's hours come from.
  • Sort 30 item cards into need, want, or gray zone; with $100 and a price list, decide what to buy, skip, and save.
  • Read a pay stub, compute net pay from gross pay, and check the percent taken out.
  • Build a monthly budget from a scenario card that pays savings first, covers every fixed need, and does not spend more than the income; check a partner's.
  • Describe seven ways to pay; write a check; keep a register of ten transactions to the cent; choose the safest way to pay in four situations.
  • State four differences between a debit card and a credit card; compute simple interest at three rates; compare the $25 minimum payment to $50 a month on a $500 balance and recommend a plan with numbers.
  • Compute compound growth on the same $100 and explain why it beats simple interest.
  • Trade in the Skittles market, track the fees, and say who got paid.
  • Take an investor number, sort the eight company cards from safest to riskiest, make first buys on the paper ledger with a written reason, and keep the Investor Journal every round.
  • Read a dispatch, predict the price move, and check the prediction.
  • Rebalance or hold when one slice of the portfolio is bigger than half, and say why.
  • Fill the sell, hold, buy decision grid before the crash round (Round 7, October 1929) and follow it.
  • Compute the percent return on the portfolio and on one company with the formula written out.
  • Write the Investor Report from the journal and the ledger, revise the personal budget with a savings line at the top, and check a partner's report.
  • Rate six soft skills honestly with evidence and write a growth plan for one.
  • Pick a top three from the 16 clusters matched to the interest code, research one career on a card with a source, and name the path from grade 8 to the job.

5. Unit objectives

One per topic, upper half of Bloom's:

  • Topic 2.1: Students will evaluate their own values, traits, strengths, and interests using a value card sort, a strengths inventory, and an interest quiz, and create a one-page All About Me that presents the results to a partner with evidence.
  • Topic 2.2: Students will design a Goal Plan for a real personal goal by writing a SMART goal and a stretch goal, applying the five-step decision model to the choice behind it, and justifying where the time and energy will come from.
  • Topic 2.3: Students will analyze a pay stub, a scenario budget, seven ways to pay, two credit payment plans, and a savings table, and create a balanced monthly personal budget that they revise at the end of the unit.
  • Topic 2.4: Students will make and justify investing decisions on the Gilded Age Market across an eight-round run from 1870 to 1932 that includes the crash of October 1929, compute the percent return, and evaluate their own decisions in an Investor Report with evidence from the Investor Journal and the ledger.
  • Topic 2.5: Students will assess their own employability skills and interests against the 16 career clusters, select a top three, and create a career research card with the path from grade 8 to one career.

The Gilded Age Market run, as the lessons set it. The market opens at Round 0 (1870) on day 53 (Lesson 2.14 Day 1): every student takes an investor number and $1,000 of paper money and makes the first buys on the ledger, and Round 1 (1873, the Panic of 1873) lands at the end of that period. The market is frozen at Round 8 at the end of day 60; Lesson 2.19 Day 1 is the closing bell at 1936 prices, the leaderboard by investor number, and the reveal (page 5 of the kit: what happened next to each company), and Day 2 is the report. That eight-round run and its close are what Rubric 02 scores. Then Round 2, as Project 02 lays it out: Lessons 2.20, 2.21, and 2.22 each open with a ten minute replay block on a fresh ledger with a fresh $1,000, replaying 1925 to 1936 with everyone knowing what is coming: the 1925 buys on day 63, the crash at Round 7 prices on day 64, the 1932 prices and then the close at 1936 on day 65. It counts as daily work (the three short Round 2 lines in the journal) and participation, not toward the rubric. The last block closes at the reset cue in Lesson 2.22 before the test; the Round 2 standings go up by investor number in the first 10 minutes of Unit 3 Day 1 (day 66), with two or three "sentence to my future self" lines from Entry 10 read aloud.

8. Project

Name: The Gilded Age Market Investor Report and Personal Budget. Full sheet in Project 02 - The Gilded Age Market Investor Report and Personal Budget.md (handed out in Lesson 2.14 and reread in 2.19); rubric in Rubric 02 - Investor Report and Personal Budget.md. The market's rules, price table, dispatches, company cards and answer key are in Handout 02.14 - The Gilded Age Market Kit.md (teacher); the student's instrument for the whole run is Handout 02.19 - Paper Ledger.md.

The scenario: It is 1870. Every student is an investor with $1,000 of paper money and an investor number; no name touches the board. The market runs eight rounds, one per class period, from 1870 to 1932, and closes in 1936. On the board are the savings bank (3 percent a round, never falls) and, as they arrive in history, RAIL, OIL, GAS, ELEC, STEEL, AUTO and RADIO. Each round the student values the ledger at the new prices, hears the dispatch (the Panic of 1873, the Standard Oil trust, the Panic of 1893, US Steel and the trustbusters, the breakup of Standard Oil, the 1920s boom with margin loans), decides what to buy, sell, or hold, and writes one Investor Journal entry saying why. Round 7 is October 1929: the crash and the margin calls. Round 8 is the bottom of 1932, and the market freezes there. On the closing bell the 1936 prices go up, the leaderboard goes up by investor number, then the reveal: what happened next to each real company (page 5 of the kit) and the lesson of each. Then the student writes it up on the report template and revises the personal budget from Lesson 2.9 with a savings line at the top.

Deliverables: (1) the Investor Journal, Entries 1 to 10, one per class day plus the three short Round 2 lines, stapled behind the report; (2) the Investor Report on Handout 02.19 - Investor Report Template.md, seven parts: starting and closing total; percent return with the formula written out; three decisions with a ledger number each and what happened; what the crash taught me; one thing I would do differently; the reveal table (what happened next to each company); the revised personal budget with a savings line; (3) the sell, hold, buy decision grid from 2.17 and the Return Math handout from 2.18, stapled behind; (4) a peer read with three boxes checked and two comments, one specific.

Steps by day: Day 1 (Lesson 2.14, day 53): saving versus investing and the risk ladder, the company cards sorted safest to riskiest, investor numbers, the first buys on the ledger at 1870 prices (Round 0); Round 1 (1873, the Panic of 1873) lands at the end of the period. Day 2 (2.14, day 54): reading the ledger (shares times price, cash, bank, total), Entry 2; Round 2 (1882, the oil trust and Edison). Days 3 and 4 (2.15, days 55 and 56): why prices move, supply and demand, the three tests on a headline, then the tests applied to the dispatches; Round 3 (1893, the Panic) and Round 4 (1901: US Steel, the trustbusters, The Landlord's Game). Day 5 (2.16, day 57): three portfolios and a shock (the Panic of 1893 the class just lived through), the pie sketch, rebalance or hold; Round 5 (1911: the breakup of Standard Oil, GM and RCA arrive). Days 6 and 7 (2.17, days 58 and 59): Round 6 (1928, the boom, the margin line opens) and the sell, hold, buy decision grid taught before the crash; then Round 7 (October 1929, the crash, margin calls), signs of stress, who sold and who held. Day 8 (2.18, day 60): Round 8 (1932, the bottom): buying low, rebalancing, the percent return formula, my own return; the market freezes at Round 8. Day 9 (2.19, day 61): the closing bell at 1936 prices, the leaderboard by investor number, the reveal, three ledger paths, Entry 9. Day 10 (2.19, day 62): write the report, revise the budget, peer read, submit; part 7 may finish at the start of Lesson 2.20. Round 2 (2.20 to 2.22, days 63 to 65): the replay of 1925 to 1936 on a fresh ledger with a fresh $1,000, ten minutes at the start of each period (1925 buys; the crash; 1932, then the close at 1936), with three short Round 2 lines in the journal; the Round 2 standings go up by investor number in the first 10 minutes of Unit 3 Day 1.

Group or individual: individual portfolio and individual report. Each student has a trading partner who checks the return math and does the peer read. Partners are the same pairs as the Skittles trades where that worked.

Choice built in: which companies to hold and how many shares; hold or rebalance at every round; whether to borrow on the margin line in Round 6; the form of the report (written on the template, typed, dictated to a partner, or recorded as a voice note with the template numbers on paper); which three decisions to write about; whether the revised budget's savings line goes to a savings account first and a slow, spread-out investment second, or to a goal from Lesson 2.5.

ELL and IEP supports: sentence starters for the journal and the report in Spanish (Vocabulary 02.md and the template boxes); the reveal slides with one picture per company; the return math as a fill-in with the subtraction line and the division line in separate boxes; calculators for all; a teacher copy of the ledger totals by round in place of a lost journal; three decisions can be two; extended time into the Round 2 block; a seat away from the leaderboard on the closing bell if rank is a stressor; the report may be written in the first language with the numbers in English, to be verified by a native speaker for scoring.

How it is presented: the closing bell debrief has two rules said before anyone speaks: nobody is named unless they name themselves, and nobody says "loser" about anyone. Two or three volunteers from the top and the bottom say what they did in one sentence. Three anonymized ledger paths, copied from the collected ledgers by investor number (one safe, one risky, one that sold at the bottom), are the texts the class reads. The grade is the rubric, not the rank: a student who finished last with a diversified portfolio, a calm crisis grid, correct math, and an honest report scores higher than a student who got lucky. I say that on day 1 and again on the closing bell.

The game, stated once (teacher facing)

Every lesson in this topic refers back to this section instead of repeating it. The whole design (the companies, the rules, the price table, the eight dispatches, the company cards, the answer key and the Round 2 replay prices) is in Handout 02.14 - The Gilded Age Market Kit.md. That file is the source; every number here matches it.

The Gilded Age Market is a class game played on paper, set in American history from 1870 to 1936. There is no website, no login and nothing stored anywhere. Each student is an investor with $1,000 of paper money and an investor number (a number on the ledger, never a name). The market runs eight rounds, one per class period, and closes in 1936. Students can hold the savings bank (BANK, pays 3 percent of the balance at the start of every round, never falls) and seven companies that arrive when they arrived in history: Pennsylvania Railroad (RAIL), Standard Oil (OIL), Manhattan Gas Light, later Consolidated Gas (GAS), Edison Electric Light, later General Electric (ELEC, from Round 2), United States Steel (STEEL, from Round 4), General Motors (AUTO, from Round 5) and the Radio Corporation of America (RADIO, from Round 5). Each round, I write the year and the prices on the board, students update the paper ledger, I read the round's news dispatch aloud, and trading opens for a few minutes: whole shares, at the round's price, cash never below zero, every trade in the trade log. The prices follow what really happened: the Panic of 1873, the oil trust of 1882, the Panic of 1893, US Steel and the trustbusters in 1901, the breakup of Standard Oil in 1911, the boom of 1928 (when the margin line opens, for that round only), the crash of October 1929 (when margin loans are called), the bottom of 1932, and the closing bell at 1936 prices. Then the reveal: what happened next to each real company, from kit page 5. The leaderboard goes up by investor number. My earlier fifteen-day plan ended with a reflection essay scored on a 16-point rubric (participation, analysis and decision making, emotional control, reflection quality); this project keeps those four criteria, adds the revised personal budget as a fifth, and fits the run into ten lesson days plus three ten-minute Round 2 blocks.

The scenario

It is 1870. You are an investor with $1,000 you did not have yesterday. Over eight rounds, one per class, you will buy and sell the companies that built the country (railroads, oil, gas, electricity, steel, cars and radio), read the news of each year, live through two panics, a boom and the crash of 1929, and find out at the closing bell in 1936 what happened next to every company you owned. Then you write the Investor Report: what you did, why, what it cost or earned, and what it changed about your own budget.

The leaderboard is real and I will read the top five by investor number. The grade is not the leaderboard. The grade is whether you can explain your decisions with numbers, whether you kept your head in the crash, and whether you took what you learned back to your own money.

How a round runs (about 10 minutes inside each lesson)

  1. I write the round's year and the new prices on the board. I do not read the news yet.
  2. You write the new prices in your ledger's price table and work out what you hold at those prices: shares times price for each company, plus cash, plus the bank. That is your total.
  3. I read the round's dispatch aloud. It says what happened that year and why.
  4. Trading opens, about four minutes. You may buy or sell any company that is on the board, at the round's price, in whole shares, and you may move money into or out of the bank. Every trade is one line in the trade log. Nobody has to trade. Holding is a decision.
  5. At the start of the next round, the bank pays 3 percent of whatever is in it, rounded to the nearest dollar.

The three rules that never change. Whole shares only. You cannot spend cash you do not have. Every trade goes in the log with the round, the company, buy or sell, the shares, the price and the cash that moved, or it did not happen.

The margin rule, Round 6 only. In 1928 a broker would lend you money to buy stock. In Round 6 the broker lends you up to the amount of cash you hold, at Round 6 prices, and the loan is written on the ledger's margin line. In Round 7 the broker wants the money back in cash. If you do not have it, you sell shares at Round 7 prices until you do, and if your shares are not enough the loan takes everything and your total is $0. That is what a margin call was.

Round 2: the replay, with the truth

After the reveal, the market replays 1925 to 1936 in three ten-minute blocks at the start of Lessons 2.20, 2.21 and 2.22. Fresh $1,000 on the Round 2 ledger. Same rules, same board, same investor number. The one difference is you: you know the crash is coming and you know what happened next to every company.

  • Block 1 (Lesson 2.20): 1925. Prices are the Round 6 prices less a fifth: RAIL 56, OIL 56, GAS 32, ELEC 72, STEEL 72, AUTO 48, RADIO 80. Buy. The margin line is open in this block, on the same rule as Round 6.
  • Block 2 (Lesson 2.21): October 1929. The Round 7 prices. Margin loans are called. Sell, hold or buy.
  • Block 3 (Lesson 2.22): 1932, then the close. The Round 8 prices; trade. Then the 1936 closing prices go up and the replay closes.

Round 2 has its own small leaderboard, posted by investor number at the start of the next class (Lesson 3.1). It is not on the rubric; the three Round 2 journal lines count as daily work, and nothing in Round 2 changes the report you already handed in. The question Round 2 asks is the one adults never get to test: knowing the crash is coming, do you still buy on margin, and do you buy at the bottom?

Team debriefs

Your ledger and your report are yours alone. Three times you work with others:

  • Pairs for the News Card match (Day 3), the fact or hype sort (Day 4), the Three Portfolios math (Day 5), the Part C comparison (Day 8), and the peer read (Day 10). A partner checks your work; a partner does not trade for you.
  • Four corners on Day 7: everyone who sold some, sold everything, held, or bought more stands together and says why. Nobody's number is read unless they read it.
  • The class debrief on Day 9: three ledger paths, by investor number only, and volunteers.

Where you get to choose

  • What you buy and when. Every trade in eight rounds is yours. The only rules are the Round 0 rules (two companies, $100 in the bank or cash) and the Round 6 and Round 7 rule (a written decision on the grid before you trade).
  • Borrow or not in Round 6. The margin line is open to everyone and nobody has to use it. The grid is where you show me your reason either way.
  • Sell, hold, or buy in the crash and at the bottom. All three can be right. The grid and the journal are where you show me your reason.
  • The three decisions in your report. Pick the three you can explain best, not the three that look best.
  • How you write the report. Handwritten on the template, typed, dictated to a partner and checked, or recorded as a voice note with the numbers written on the template. The seven parts are the same in every form.
  • The savings line. Add one, raise one, or split one into an emergency cushion and a long-term line. Your budget, your number, your reason.

Running the market on paper (teacher facing)

The teacher is the market. The whole run needs the kit, the board, one ledger per student, calculators (one per pair is enough), and the company cards cut apart (one set per pair) for Day 1.

What you need. Handout 02.14 - The Gilded Age Market Kit.md (pages 1 to 3 and 5 stay with you; page 4 is cut into cards), Handout 02.19 - Paper Ledger.md (one per student, in the FACS folder from Round 0 to the close), Handout 02.14 - Investor Journal.md, the board, and a calculator per pair.

Before each round. Write the year and the prices on the board before students walk in, or at the start of the round step in the lesson. Do not read the dispatch until the ledgers are updated. The kit's price table is the only source; do not round further or "make it exciting." The direction of every move is history.

The bank. Pays 3 percent of the balance at the start of Rounds 1 to 8, eight times, rounded to the nearest dollar. It does not pay at the closing bell. (The kit says "the start of every round"; the close is not a round. If you want a ninth payment at the bell, say so on Day 1 and change the ledger's rule line.)

The margin line, Round 6. Say the rule once, clearly, from kit page 1, and do not talk anyone out of borrowing. Walk the room during trading and initial every margin loan so the amount cannot change later. In Round 7, settle every loan before trading opens, with you watching: cash paid, or shares sold at Round 7 prices in the trade log, or the ledger marked $0. A student at $0 has nothing left, bank included (the kit says the loan takes everything). They keep the ledger, write Entry 7 and Entry 8 about it, and get the fresh $1,000 in Round 2 like everyone else. Say that on Day 6 so nobody is surprised.

Collecting the ledgers. Collect them at the bell every round, or let students keep them in the folder if the class can be trusted with its own prices. Spot check one ledger a day: pick a trade in the log and confirm shares times price equals the cash change; then confirm that round's total equals cash plus the bank plus shares times the board price for each line. The Paper Ledger's teacher key has a worked check.

The leaderboard. At the closing bell, the teacher (or two student clerks, by investor number only) lists every closing total on the board, sorted. Read the top five and stop.

Round 2 on paper. The replay uses the Round 2 section of the same ledger: a fresh $1,000, the 1925 prices from kit page 2, then the Round 7, Round 8 and Close columns as printed. Same routine, ten minutes a block.
