Enduring understanding and essential question

Big idea: The market on the board was the real country with the prices rounded. What you did with paper dollars over eight rounds is what adults do with real money for fifty years, and the habits are the same: save first, spread it out, read the news slowly, do not borrow to buy what is already expensive, and do not sell because you are scared.
Essential question: Now that you know what happened next to every company on the board, what would you tell yourself in 1870?

Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. (Low) Given the reveal, state for each of the seven companies what happened next and the lesson of each, and state what the bank and Monopoly stand for.
  2. (Mid) Given their own ledger and journal, analyze three of their decisions (what, why, what happened) and compute their starting total, closing total at 1936 prices, and percent return for the Investor Report.
  3. (High) Given their personal budget from Lesson 2.9 and what the market taught them, revise the budget with a savings line and defend the revision in the report.

Vocabulary

Tier 2 (general academic): reveal, represent, revise, defend
Tier 3 (FACS): closing bell, leaderboard, dividend, bankruptcy, trust, monopoly, deposit insurance, savings line. See the unit vocabulary list for the Spanish column.

The ledgers were collected at the end of 2.18. Day 2 is a writing day: the report is written from the journal and the ledger.

Pre-assessment

The Lesson 2.18 exit cards tell you who has a correct percent return in hand and who needs the worked-example card before writing. The journal count (how many of Entries 1 to 8 are complete) tells you who will struggle to find three decisions on Day 2; give those students their own ledger's trade log as the source, and one of the printed example paths if the ledger is thin.

Do now and hook (Time: 3 min, Day 1)

On the board (closing prices still covered): "Guess your final rank in this class (top third, middle, bottom third). Then one sentence: what one decision decided it."

Debrief: hands for each third. Then the hook: uncover the 1936 prices. "That is the closing bell. Nobody trades again on this board. In ten minutes I will tell you what happened next to every company you owned."

RAIL, Pennsylvania Railroad: survived every panic, paid dividends for more than a century, went bankrupt in 1970 after merging with the New York Central, the largest bankruptcy the country had seen; big and old is not the same as forever. OIL, Standard Oil: broken into 34 companies in 1911; the pieces became Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco and others; a company can be bigger than the government wants and still be a good business. GAS, Consolidated Gas: became Consolidated Edison, which still sends New York its gas and electric bills; boring and necessary pays every month. ELEC, General Electric: nearly died in 1893, then lit the country; one of the twelve original companies in the Dow Jones average of 1896 and in it for over a century; a new technology's stock can crash and still be right about the future. AUTO, General Motors: the largest company in the world by the 1950s, bankrupt in 2009, rescued and reorganized; the best company of one era is not the best of the next. Questions to ask

Monitor understanding:

  • Which company on the board still sends New York a bill? Which one went bankrupt in 1970? Which one was broken up and made its owners richer?
  • Your closing total is $1,240. Your percent return is what, and how did you get it?
  • Where is the savings line on your revised budget: before or after the wants?

Deepen learning:

  • The bank never lost a dollar in this game. In real life, about nine thousand of them failed between 1930 and 1933. What changed in 1933, and why does that matter for the savings line you are about to write?
  • RADIO went from 6 to 100 to 3. Someone in this room owned it. What would you have needed to know in 1928 to sell it at 100, and could anyone have known it?
  • Lizzie Magie made a game to warn people about monopolies, and it was sold as a game about building one. What does that tell you about who tells the story of money?
  • What is the one thing you would tell yourself in 1870?

Check for understanding

Day 1, Step 2: clipboard yes or no per student on "reveal table has all seven companies with what happened next." Day 2, Step 6: yes or no on "formula written in part 2" and "three decisions each have a ledger number." Day 2, Step 7: yes or no on "savings line present and the budget balances." A "got it" report reads, in part 3: "Entry 7: I sold 4 RADIO at 30 because I owed the broker $200 and had no cash. RADIO went to 3 in Round 8, so selling saved me $108, but I never bought anything at the bottom, so I missed AUTO going from 8 to 60."

Closure (Time: 4 min Day 1, 3 min Day 2)

Day 1: 3-2-1 exit card: 3 companies and what happened next, 2 things the investors who did best did, 1 thing you would tell yourself in 1870. Three students read the "1" aloud. Sort; anyone with fewer than three companies gets the reveal slides printed for Day 2.
Day 2: Investor Journal Entry 10, two lines: "My final percent return was ___. One sentence to my future self about money." Two students read the sentence aloud. The report is the summative check for Topic 2.4; there is no exit card sort, because the rubric does that work.

Differentiation and supports

  • ELL: the reveal slides have one picture each (a locomotive, an oil derrick, a gas street lamp, a light bulb, a steel bridge, a Model T, a radio set) next to the company name and the lesson in one short line; the report template is boxes with a sentence starter in each, in English and Spanish where the starter is short ("I started with ___ and ended with ___," "The crash taught me ___," "Next time I will ___"); the budget revision table is the same shape as the 2.9 budget; a student may write the report in their first language and give the numbers in English, with the translated version to be verified by a native speaker for scoring.
  • IEP and 504 (general): the report can be typed, dictated to a partner, or recorded as a voice note with the template numbers filled in on paper; extended time by finishing part 7 at the start of Lesson 2.20 (the Round 2 block gives a natural ten minutes); the three decisions can be two; the ledger's trade log replaces a lost journal; seat the student away from the board on Day 1 if rank is a stressor.
  • Grade 6 support: parts 3 and 5 use the sentence starters as the whole answer; the reveal table has the "what happened next" lines in a word bank to match; the budget revision is one change (add or raise the savings line) with the wants adjusted by the teacher's suggestion.
  • Grade 8 stretch: part 3 includes, for each decision, what a diversified investor would have done instead; part 7 splits the savings line into an emergency cushion and a long-term line, and names where each would go and why, using the reveal (an insured savings account for the cushion; a spread of companies for the long line); the student writes one paragraph on luck vs habit over eight rounds vs fifty years.
  • UDL checkpoint used: multiple means of action and expression (written, typed, dictated, or recorded report; boxes instead of an essay) and multiple means of engagement (the student's own ledger is the text, and the revised budget is about their own money).
  • No-kitchen or no-machine alternative: not needed; both days are paper and a board. If the projector is out, the reveal slides are read aloud from kit page 5 and the three paths are written on the board.

Homework

None. The report is finished in class. A student who needs more time finishes part 7 during the Round 2 block of Lesson 2.20.

Connections

Community: a bank or credit union education officer, or a parent who works in finance, can sit in on Day 1 for the reveal and take three questions; they see a class that can say what deposit insurance is and why it exists, and the class hears an adult say "I held when it fell." ELA: the report is a piece of evidence-based writing (claim, number, reason); the ELA teacher may accept it as a writing sample if the two classes agree. Social studies: the reveal is a one-page history of American business from 1870 to 1936; the social studies teacher may want the kit.

Teacher notes

  • If Day 1 runs short, cut Step 3 to the three printed paths and skip the volunteers; the volunteers' stories come back in the report anyway. Never cut the reveal; it is the whole point of eight rounds of history.
  • If Day 2 runs short, the peer read becomes a self-check with the same three boxes, and part 7 finishes in Lesson 2.20's Round 2 block.
  • The leaderboard is the moment to be careful. Read the top five by investor number. Do not read the bottom. A student who is last knows it; the class does not need to. Anyone wiped out by a margin call in Round 7 shows a closing total of $0 and that is a real number on the board; say once that the report scores the reasoning, and move on.
  • The honest part of Step 3: on this price table, the investor who sold everything in October 1929 and sat in the bank finished a little ahead of the one who never sold, because 1936 prices had not come back to 1929. Do not hide it. The lesson is not "never sell." It is: selling at the bottom was the disaster, borrowing at the top was the disaster, and buying at the bottom was the best decision on the board. Then say the reveal's line about the bank: the seller's money was safe in the game and would not have been in real life until 1933.
  • My earlier plan ended with a one to two page reflection essay. The report template replaces it with seven boxed parts so the same thinking fits in one period and scores against a rubric. If a student wants to write the essay instead, the template's parts 3 to 5 are the outline.
  • The Round 2 replay during Lessons 2.20 to 2.22 runs on the same board after the reveal, with a fresh $1,000 and everyone knowing what is coming; see Project 02 for what changes (nothing in the rules, everything in what students know).
