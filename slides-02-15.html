Lesson 2.15, two days. Day 1: slides 1 to 9. Day 2: slides 10 to 18. Every dispatch line is from page 3 of Handout 02.14 - The Gilded Age Market Kit.md; the hype headlines on slides 10 and 11 are mine, written for the game.

Slide 1

Do now (Day 1)

  • The Round 2 dispatch, 1882, shown in full.
  • Read it again. Write: what happened, and which companies care.

Teacher notes

4 minutes. Cold call three for the companies: OIL, ELEC, RAIL. Then point at the price table.

Slide 2

Why did those prices move?

  • RAIL: 50, 30, 45. OIL: 10, 12, 30.
  • Nobody in this room made those numbers.
  • A great many people heard news like this and decided the same thing at the same time.
  • Today: read the news the way they did, and know when the news is lying.

Teacher notes

Image: a crowd of men in hats around a newspaper stand, 1880s, one pointing at the front page.

Slide 3

Supply and demand in one picture

  • A company has a set number of shares.
  • Lots of buyers, few sellers: the price goes up.
  • Lots of sellers, few buyers: the price goes down.
  • That is the whole rule.

Teacher notes

Image: a seesaw. Left side labeled BUYERS with many figures, right side SELLERS with two; the price arrow tilts up.

Copy the two lines into the journal notes box.

Slide 4

Four kinds of news you have already heard

  • A bank fails and the borrowing stops: RAIL fell from 50 to 30 in 1873.
  • People still need light: OIL and GAS rose a little in the same panic.
  • One company controls almost all of a thing: OIL rose from 12 to 30 in 1882.
  • A new invention arrives: ELEC joined the board at 25 in 1882.
  • The bank ignores the news.

Teacher notes

Image: four small drawings: a closed bank door, a lit kerosene lamp, an oil barrel with a crown on it, a light bulb.

Slide 5

The three tests for a headline

  • Who says it? A named company, a court, Congress, a dated report passes. "Everyone" or "insiders" or nobody fails.
  • Is there a number? A date, a percent, a count, an amount passes. No number fails.
  • Does it tell you to hurry? No rush words passes. "Now," "last chance," "before it is too late" fails.
  • Two or three passes: probably a fact. Zero or one: hype.

Teacher notes

Image: three icons in a row: a mouth, a number sign, a running figure with a red X.

Copy the tests. Tomorrow we use them. Today we only learn them.

Slide 6

Part A: match the news to the price

  • Eight pieces of the dispatches from 1873 and 1882. Eight price moves from the board, shuffled.
  • Match each card to the move it caused.
  • Write the sentence: "___ happened, so more people wanted to buy / sell ___, so the price went up / down."
  • Pairs. 8 minutes.

Teacher notes

Circulate with the clipboard: six of eight matched, direction named. Debrief Jay Cooke and RAIL, then the two cards about light.

Slide 7

Round 3. The year is 1893.

  • Before the prices go up: one prediction on Entry 3. Which company on my ledger is most at risk if there is another panic, and why? Use the card.
  • Then: pay the bank, fill the column, work out your total. Then the dispatch.
  • Question from the dispatch: a friend who put every dollar into railroads, and a friend who split it four ways. Which one is still standing?

Teacher notes

Image: a locked bank door with a crowd outside it, 1893.

10 minutes. Prices: RAIL 25, OIL 28, GAS 24, ELEC 10. Read the Round 3 dispatch after the totals are done. Four minutes of trading.

Closure (Day 1)

Slide 10

Do now (Day 2)

  • Headline A: "In February 1893 the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad, one of the biggest, goes bankrupt."
  • Headline B: "RAILROADS ARE FINISHED. Everyone is selling. Get out NOW before you lose it all."
  • Which one would you trust with your money? One reason.

Teacher notes

4 minutes. Headline A is the first line of the Round 3 dispatch. Headline B is mine.

Slide 11

Score the do now

  • Headline A: a named railroad, a date, no hurry. Three. Fact.
  • Headline B: nobody, no number, "NOW." Zero. Hype.
  • A headline that tells you to hurry was written by someone who wants your money to move today.

Teacher notes

Say the last line slowly. It is the real-life rule.

Slide 12

One from the present

  • A post online: "This stock is going to the moon. Everyone is buying. Do not miss it."
  • Who says it? Is there a number? Does it tell you to hurry?
  • Same test. Same answer. 1893 or today.

Teacher notes

Image: a drawn phone screen with a rocket post and no name on the account.

No real company names. Students will supply their own; do not confirm any.

Slide 13

Part B: fact or hype sort

  • Six headlines from 1893. Three are pieces of the real dispatches. Three are made up to sound urgent.
  • Score each on the three tests. Label fact or hype.
  • For each fact: what would a careful investor do? Buy, sell, hold, or wait for more.
  • Pairs. 9 minutes.
Slide 14

Debrief: headline 3

  • "General Electric, formed only last year when Edison's company merged with a rival, watches its stock fall to a fraction of its price."
  • Fact. Bad news. And a careful investor might hold, or even buy a little.
  • Why? The stock fell. The dispatch does not say the company closed.

Teacher notes

Image: an early light bulb, lit, on a dark background.

Slide 15

Round 4, 1901. The first billion dollar company.

  • Pay the bank first. Fill the column: RAIL 60, OIL 40, GAS 30, ELEC 40, and a new line, STEEL 40.
  • Work out your total. Then the dispatch.
  • One investor runs the three tests on the dispatch's first sentence out loud.
  • Question from the dispatch: US Steel is brand new and enormous. Is new and enormous the same as safe?

Teacher notes

Image: a steel mill at night with sparks, 1901.

10 minutes. Read the Round 4 dispatch after the totals are done. Four minutes of trading.

Slide 16

Entry 4

Teacher notes

6 minutes.

Slide 17

The rule you keep

  • Prices first. Then the story. Then the decision.
  • Two of three tests failed: it is hype. Do nothing.
  • Nobody has to trade. Holding is a decision.

Teacher notes

Image: a ledger page, a folded newspaper, and a pencil.

Slide 18

Closure (Day 2)

  • 3-2-1: 3 tests for hype, 2 headlines you labeled today, 1 rule you will use with real news.
  • Three investors read the "1."
  • Tomorrow: eggs and baskets.

Teacher notes

4 minutes. Fewer than three tests named is "needs more."
