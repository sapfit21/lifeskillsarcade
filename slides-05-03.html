  • Sort them into two lists. Guess if you do not know.
  • Two minutes

Slide 3

The answer, and the tricky one

  • Natural: cotton, wool, silk, linen
  • Manufactured: polyester, nylon, rayon, spandex
  • Rayon starts as wood pulp, which is a plant, but it is made into fiber in a factory. It counts as manufactured.

Teacher notes

Image: a cotton boll, a sheep, a silkworm cocoon, and a flax plant on the left; a factory with spools coming out of it on the right.

Slide 4

One of these is a plastic bottle

  • A fleece jacket and a wool sweater
  • Both keep you warm
  • One is made from plastic. One is made from a haircut.
  • By the end of today you can tell them apart with your hands

Slide 5

Natural fibers, what each one is good and bad at

  • Cotton: soft, absorbent, breathable. Wrinkles. Shrinks. Slow to dry.
  • Wool: warm even when damp, springs back. Itchy to some people. Hot water felts it.
  • Silk: strong and smooth, costly. Water can mark it. Hand wash.
  • Linen: coolest in heat, gets softer with age. Wrinkles the most.

Teacher notes

Image: four swatches labeled cotton, wool, silk, linen with a small source icon on each.

Slide 6

Manufactured fibers, what each one is good and bad at

  • Polyester: strong, holds shape, dries fast, cheap. Does not breathe. Holds odor. Melts.
  • Nylon: very strong and slick. Windbreakers and tights. Melts.
  • Rayon: drapes like silk, from wood pulp. Weak when wet.
  • Spandex: stretches and comes back, always blended. Heat destroys it.

Teacher notes

Image: four swatches labeled polyester, nylon, rayon, spandex with a small factory icon on each.

Slide 8

The three tests

  1. Feel: rub it between finger and thumb. Smooth or rough? Warm or cool? Thick or thin?
  2. Stretch: pull gently across and let go. Does it come back?
  3. Water: one drop. Count to ten. Soaked in, beaded up, or spread out?

Teacher notes

Image: three small drawings, fingers rubbing cloth, two hands pulling cloth, and a dropper releasing one drop onto cloth.

Slide 9

Station rules

  • One drop, not a puddle
  • Swatches stay at the station
  • Wet swatches go on the paper towel, letter side up
  • Ninety seconds per swatch. Six swatches.
  • Nothing goes in a mouth

Teacher notes

Image: a station setup photo: six swatches, a water cup, a dropper, a paper towel, and a sealed envelope.

Slide 10

The burn test, teacher demonstration only

  • I hold the tweezers. The flame is on for under three seconds.
  • Nobody stands up. Nobody films it.
  • The extinguisher is for adults. PASS is pull, aim at the base, squeeze, sweep. Students get out and tell the teacher.
  • Watch three things: what the flame does, the smell, what is left behind

Teacher notes

Image: a metal pie pan on a table with long tweezers holding a small fabric snip, and a taped line on the floor with chairs behind it.

Same fire rules, same words, as Lesson 1.3. Tell the class the day before that there will be a flame, so anyone who needs to sit out can tell you quietly.

Slide 11

Three fibers, three results

  • Cotton: burns fast, smells like burning paper, soft gray ash that crumbles
  • Wool: burns slowly, curls away, smells like burning hair, a black bead you can crush, often puts itself out
  • Polyester: does not burn, it melts and shrinks away, smells sweet and chemical, leaves a hard bead
  • A fiber that melts sticks to skin. That is why firefighters and welders do not wear fleece.

Teacher notes

Image: three small results side by side, gray crumbly ash, a black crushable bead, and a hard shiny bead.

If the building says no flame: play the same three burns on video, then run the wrinkle test (fist for ten seconds, rate 1 to 3) and make the melt-versus-burn point from the video.
