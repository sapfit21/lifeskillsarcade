Lesson 2.19, two days. Day 1: slides 1 to 14. Day 2: slides 15 to 22. The market is frozen at Round 8. Write "Closing bell, 1936" and the closing prices on the board before Day 1 from page 2 of Handout 02.14 - The Gilded Age Market Kit.md and keep them covered until slide 2. Slides 5 to 12 are kit page 5, one company per slide. The leaderboard shows investor numbers only.

Slide 1

Do now (Day 1)

  • Guess your final rank in this class: top third, middle, bottom third.
  • One sentence: what one decision decided it.

Teacher notes

3 minutes. Hands for each third. Then uncover the board.

Slide 2

The closing bell, 1936

  • RAIL 30. OIL 60. GAS 30. ELEC 40. STEEL 60. AUTO 60. RADIO 12.
  • The market did not come back to 1929 for twenty five years, but it climbed a long way from the bottom.
  • In 1933 the government closed every bank for a week, reopened the sound ones, and created deposit insurance.
  • In 1935 Parker Brothers began selling Monopoly.
  • Fill the Close column and the closing table. No trades. No interest at the bell.

Teacher notes

Image: a drawn brass bell over a 1936 newspaper front page.

Read the closing bell dispatch from kit page 3. Then call investor numbers and write every closing total on the board.

Slide 3

The top five

  • Investor ___: $______. Investor ___: $______. Investor ___: $______. Investor ___: $______. Investor ___: $______.
  • Class average closing total: $______. Class average return: ______ percent.
  • Two rules: nobody is named unless they name themselves; nobody says "loser" about anyone.

Teacher notes

Read the top five and stop.

Slide 4

Your line

  • Find your investor number. Write your closing total and your rank on the report template cover.
  • Who is willing to say what they did?

Teacher notes

7 minutes for slides 2 to 4.

Slide 5

The reveal: RAIL, Pennsylvania Railroad

  • Survived every panic. Paid dividends for more than a century.
  • Went bankrupt in 1970 after merging with the New York Central, the largest bankruptcy the country had seen.
  • Lesson: big and old is not the same as forever.

Teacher notes

Image: a steam locomotive on a stone bridge.

Slide 6

OIL, Standard Oil

  • Broken into 34 companies in 1911.
  • The pieces became Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco and others, and their owners grew richer than before.
  • Lesson: a company can be bigger than the government wants and still be a good business.

Teacher notes

Image: an oil derrick beside a row of kerosene lamps.

Slide 7

GAS, Consolidated Gas

  • Became Consolidated Edison, which still sends New York its gas and electric bills.
  • Lesson: boring and necessary pays every month.

Teacher notes

Image: a gas street lamp on a New York corner.

Slide 8

ELEC, General Electric

  • Nearly died in 1893, then lit the country.
  • One of the twelve original companies in the Dow Jones average of 1896, and in it for over a century.
  • Lesson: a new technology's stock can crash and still be right about the future.

Teacher notes

Image: a Pearl Street lamp glowing in a dark office window.

Slide 9

STEEL, US Steel

  • Still makes steel. Its share of the market shrank for a hundred years.
  • Lesson: enormous on the first day is not a guarantee.

Teacher notes

Image: a steel bridge under construction.

Slide 10

AUTO, General Motors

  • The largest company in the world by the 1950s. Bankrupt in 2009. Rescued and reorganized.
  • Lesson: the best company of one era is not the best of the next.

Teacher notes

Image: a Model T on a dirt road, with a newer car far behind it.

Slide 11

RADIO, RCA

  • Under $6 in 1921. Over $100 in September 1929. Under $3 in 1932.
  • It took until the 1960s to matter again. Bought and broken up in 1986.
  • Lesson: the hottest stock of a decade is usually the most expensive way to learn the word "bubble."

Teacher notes

Image: a 1920s radio set in a living room.

Students fill the reveal table on the report template as each slide goes up.

Slide 12

The bank, and Monopoly

  • About nine thousand banks failed between 1930 and 1933. Their depositors lost their money.
  • Deposit insurance, created in 1933, is why "the bank never fails" is true for you and was not for your great great grandparents.
  • Lizzie Magie patented The Landlord's Game in 1904 to teach that letting one person own everything hurts everyone else. Parker Brothers bought it in 1935 and sold it as Monopoly, a game about doing exactly that.
  • The trust on our board in Round 2 is the thing her game was about.

Teacher notes

Image: a Monopoly board next to a 1904 patent drawing.

12 minutes for slides 5 to 12.

Slide 13

Three ledgers, by investor number only

  • Investor ___ never sold: $2,618 in 1928, $926 in 1932, $1,926 at the bell.
  • Investor ___ sold everything in October 1929 and put it in the bank: $1,949 at the bell. (In real life, would the bank have been there in 1933?)
  • Investor ___ sold in the crash, then bought OIL, STEEL, AUTO and ELEC at the bottom in 1932: $5,949 at the bell.
  • The two paths nobody wants: sold at the bottom, $926. Borrowed in 1928, $0.
  • Read each one: what did they do, and what did it cost or earn?

Teacher notes

Image: three simple line graphs of a total across the ten columns: a mountain that comes most of the way back; a mountain that goes flat after 1929; a mountain that goes flat and then shoots up.

From the Paper Ledger's teacher key, or this class's real ledgers. 8 minutes with volunteers. End with: when you sold mattered more than that you sold; over eight rounds, luck matters a lot; over fifty years, habits matter more.

Slide 14

Entry 9 and closure (Day 1)

  • The market is closed. My closing total in dollars. Why my number is what it is. What I know now that I did not know in 1870.
  • 3-2-1: 3 companies and what happened next, 2 things the investors who did best did, 1 thing you would tell yourself in 1870.
  • Tomorrow: the Investor Report. Bring your journal and your ledger.

Teacher notes

6 minutes for Entry 9, 4 for closure.

Slide 15

Do now (Day 2)

  • Open your journal and your ledger's trade log. Circle the three decisions you are proudest of, or least proud of.
  • Write the entry numbers on the report template cover.

Teacher notes

3 minutes.

Slide 16

The Investor Report: seven parts

  • 1. Starting and closing total. 2. Percent return, with the formula.
  • 3. Three decisions and why, each with a ledger number. 4. What the crash taught me.
  • 5. One thing I would do differently. 6. The reveal table (done yesterday).
  • 7. My revised personal budget with a savings line.

Teacher notes

4 minutes. Point at where each rubric criterion lives on the template.

Slide 17

Where the rubric lives

  • Criterion 1, participation and journal: your ledger's trade log and your ten entries.
  • Criterion 2, analysis and decisions: part 3.
  • Criterion 3, emotional control: the grid and part 4.
  • Criterion 4, math and reflection: parts 1, 2, 5, 6.
  • Criterion 5, the revised budget: part 7.
Slide 18

Write parts 1 to 5

  • Sources: your journal, your ledger, your grid, your Return Math handout. Nothing from memory.
  • Part 2: write the formula out. Bottom of the fraction: 1,000.
  • Part 3: every decision gets a number: shares, a price, or a round.
  • 16 minutes.

Teacher notes

Circulate. A "got it" part 3 reads: "Entry 7: I sold 4 RADIO at 30 because I owed the broker $200 and had no cash. RADIO went to 3 in Round 8, so selling saved me $108, but I never bought anything at the bottom, so I missed AUTO going from 8 to 60."

Slide 19

Part 7: the revised budget

  • Pull your Lesson 2.9 budget. Copy it into the "Before" column.
  • Same income. Same needs.
  • A savings line: add one or raise one. Put it before the wants. Pay it first.
  • Adjust the wants until it balances. Two sentences: what changed and why the market made you change it.

Teacher notes

Image: the revision table with the savings line highlighted and an arrow moving it above the wants.

8 minutes. The rule that connects the topic: pay yourself first, then the wants.

Slide 20

Where the savings go

  • First: a savings account. Since 1933 it is insured. It does not drop. A cushion for a bad month.
  • Second, once the cushion is there: a spread of companies, held a long time. Time smooths the crashes; the never-sold ledger proved it.
  • Grade 8 stretch: split your savings line into the two and name why.
Slide 21

Peer read and submit

  • Swap. Check three boxes: the formula is written with 1,000 on the bottom; three decisions each have a number; the budget balances with savings before wants.
  • Two comments, one specific. Get it back. Fix one thing.
  • Staple the journal, the ledger, the grid, and Return Math behind the report. Self-assessment row. In the folder.

Teacher notes

6 minutes.

Slide 22

Closure (Day 2)

  • Entry 10: my final percent return was ___. One sentence to my future self about money.
  • Two investors read the sentence.
  • Tomorrow: careers. And ten minutes of Round 2: it is 1925, you have a fresh $1,000, and you know what is coming.

Teacher notes

3 minutes.
